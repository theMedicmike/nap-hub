# The Family Tree of Healing

*How the world's systems of medicine are connected: a walk from the oldest roots to today.*

No system of medicine grew alone. Knowledge moved along the same roads as silk, spice, pilgrims and armies. Every link here comes from a source in the fifteen chapters. Where the record is thin or historians disagree, we say so. **Legend** marks a story a tradition tells about itself. **History** marks what the written record shows. **Debated** marks a link that scholars still argue over.

This is history, not a claim that any remedy works.

---

## 1. The oldest roots: clay and papyrus

The first branches we can see are written on clay. In Sumer, someone wrote down a list of medicines more than four thousand years ago. The tablet is dated anywhere from about 2500 to 2100 BCE, depending on the scholar. **(History.)** Akkadian-speaking scholars in Babylonia and Assyria later took over these Sumerian texts. They translated and expanded them, and copied them for centuries in scribal schools and in families of healers. In the 7th century BCE, King Ashurbanipal gathered Babylonian medical texts into his library at Nineveh, which was sacked in 612 BCE.

Along the Nile, Egypt's medical papyri are just as old on paper. The Edwin Smith Papyrus dates to about 1600 BCE and the Ebers to about 1550 BCE, and both are copies of older texts. Egypt's reputation for medicine spread far. Homer's *Odyssey* calls Egypt a land rich in drugs. Herodotus says the Persian king Cyrus asked the pharaoh Amasis for his best eye doctor, and that Egyptian physicians later served at the court of Darius. **(History, as Greek writers report it.)** Egyptian ingredients such as castor oil, natron and temple plasters show up in Greek medical writing. Imhotep, an Egyptian architect and official, was later worshipped as a god of healing, and Greeks living in Egypt identified him with their own Asclepius. **(Legend built on a real person.)** Some Greek physicians even labelled their own mixtures "Egyptian" for prestige.

**Debated:** Did Babylonian medicine shape Hippocratic medicine? The island of Kos lies just off the coast of Asia Minor, and the two traditions overlapped in time. Some scholars see one shared Near Eastern medicine before Hippocrates. Others see transmission in the 9th to 7th centuries BCE. Still others see a common inheritance rather than direct borrowing. Cuneiform medicine also left traces in the Babylonian Talmud, where Akkadian loanwords and translated recipes appear, perhaps through Aramaic.

## 2. India and Greece meet, briefly

Ayurveda's roots reach back to the Vedas. Most scholars date the earliest Vedic texts to about 1500 to 1200 BCE, and the Atharvaveda took shape somewhat later. For centuries its knowledge lived in memorized verse before the great written collections of Charaka and Sushruta. **(History, with uncertain dates.)**

In about 326 BCE, Alexander's army reached the Indus. His officer Nearchus, quoted by the later writer Arrian, says Greek doctors had no cure for snakebite, so Alexander kept the most skilled Indian healers near his tent. **(History.)** This shows contact and practical help. It does not show that one system's theory passed into the other.

## 3. The Silk Road and the Buddhist road

Buddhist monks and texts carried Indian medicine north and east. Near Kucha, on the Silk Road, explorers found the Bower Manuscript, Sanskrit medical texts written on birch bark. One of them quotes Charaka's formulas. Scholars date it to about the 4th to 6th centuries CE, though that date is debated.

In China, Tang-era writers of the 7th century included prescriptions linked to Jivaka, the Indian "Medicine King" of Buddhist lore. **(Jivaka: legend. The prescriptions in Sun Simiao's books: history.)** The same Buddhist stories of Jivaka travelled in Sanskrit, Tibetan and Chinese, and a version of his skull-opening tale even surfaces in Tamil Siddha legend.

Tibet became a meeting point of three streams. Most of the *Four Tantras*, the great Tibetan classic usually dated to the 12th century, is built on Vagbhata's Indian *Ashtanga Hridaya*. From China came pulse reading and moxibustion, which already appear in the Dunhuang manuscripts. From Persia and the Islamic world, along the musk-trade routes, came words for theriac, camphor and saffron, and a style of urine diagnosis that closely resembles Ibn Sina's. A thinner trace of Greek (Galenic) medicine arrived indirectly. The story of a Greek physician called "Galenos" at the Tibetan court is first written down in the 16th century. **(Legend.)** Tibetan medicine then branched again. Gelug monasteries carried it to Mongolia in the 17th century, where more than a hundred monastery medical colleges taught it, and on to the Buryats of Siberia. From 1961, Tibetan refugees rebuilt it in exile at Dharamsala, and India recognized Sowa Rigpa by law in 2010.

## 4. China's branches: Korea and Japan

Chinese medical texts and physicians reached Korea by the 5th to 6th centuries CE, and Korea became the bridge to Japan. Japanese records mention a Korean physician in 459 and Chinese medical books arriving through Korea in 562, though some accounts date the start to the 7th century. **(Debated dating.)** In 984 the court physician Tamba Yasuyori finished the *Ishinpō*, which preserves Chinese texts later lost in China itself.

Japan kept returning to China for new ideas, from Tashiro Sanki's Jin-Yuan theory to the Shanghan lun revival behind Yoshimasu Todo. Knowledge also flowed back. Korea's *Dongui Bogam* (1613) was reprinted in Japan and more than thirty times in China. Then Dutch anatomy arrived through Nagasaki in 1774. In 1804 Hanaoka Seishu, inspired by accounts of the ancient surgeon Hua Tuo's lost anaesthetic, operated under a herbal general anaesthetic, 42 years before the famous ether demonstration in Boston. **(History. Hua Tuo's formula itself is lost.)** Chinese medicine also spread into Vietnam and Southeast Asia, and in the 1960s it reached Ghana, when Dr. Oku Ampofo was sent to study herbal medicine in China.

## 5. The great translation: Gondishapur, Baghdad, and on to Europe

In southwestern Iran, Gondishapur brought Greek medicine (read in Syriac) together with Persian and Indian learning. Some historians think later writers made its school sound grander than it was, but Gondishapur-trained physicians are well attested. Under Caliph Harun al-Rashid (786–809), Jibrail ibn Bukhtishu of a Gondishapur family helped found what is often called Baghdad's first hospital.

Baghdad became the great workshop of translation. Hunayn ibn Ishaq (809–873), with his son and nephew, turned Galen and Hippocrates into Arabic, often through Syriac. At the same time, under the Barmakid viziers, the Charaka and Sushruta collections were translated into Arabic, with Indian physicians such as Manka often named, though the details are contested. Around 850, al-Tabari's *Firdaws al-Hikma* gave Indian medicine a section of its own. Greek, Persian and Indian streams mixed in Arabic, and al-Zahrawi in Córdoba built on the Byzantine surgeon Paul of Aegina.

Then the river turned west. In the late 11th century, Constantine the African translated Arabic works into Latin at Monte Cassino, feeding the famous school at nearby Salerno. (How early Salerno began is disputed.) From about 1100, translators in Toledo, above all Gerard of Cremona, put Ibn Sina's *Canon* into Latin, and European universities taught from it until the mid-1600s. Galen's humours, carried along this whole chain, ruled European medicine until the Scientific Revolution. Unani medicine also travelled east with hakims into South Asia, where it lives today beside Ayurveda and probably gave Tamil Siddha its pulse reading and urine examination. Islamic medical scholarship crossed the Sahara too, into the manuscript libraries of Timbuktu.

## 6. South India's own branch

Siddha grew from shared Sanskrit and Tamil learning. It took Ayurveda's five elements, three humours and seven tissues, with Tamil works mentioning the humours by about 450–550 CE. Tradition credits eighteen Siddhars, led by Agastya. **(Legend.)** It became a separately named system in the 1920s and 1930s, when Tamil revival and colonial committees defined it apart from Ayurveda. It reached Sri Lanka, where formal teaching began in 1929.

## 7. Europe's herbals and its folk remedies

Monks blended Mediterranean texts with local cures. *Bald's Leechbook* (c. 950) mixes Latin learning with English remedies. One passage claims that the Patriarch of Jerusalem sent remedies to King Alfred. **(Claimed; unverified.)** Hildegard of Bingen drew on classical theory in the 12th century. Printing spread the herbals: Leonhart Fuchs (1542) was copied by Bock and Dodoens, Dodoens was turned into English by Lyte and Gerard, and in 1649 Culpeper translated the physicians' Latin pharmacopoeia for ordinary readers. Ayurvedic plant knowledge reached European readers through Garcia de Orta's book, written in Portuguese Goa in the 16th century.

## 8. The Columbian exchange, and other crossings by sea

Andean bark became Europe's "Jesuit's powder" between about 1620 and 1650. Nahua healer Martín de la Cruz's herbal was translated into Latin in 1552. Amazonian and Guianan curare passed through explorers' reports and laboratories to the operating room in 1942. Plains nations' echinacea entered American Eclectic medicine in the 1880s. Khoekhoe and San buchu reached British and American pharmacopoeias in the 1820s to 1840s, and rooibos became a global tea. Forced migration carried Yoruba healing knowledge to Brazil, where it lives in Candomblé. West African inoculation reached Boston through Onesimus, an enslaved man, in 1721. The crossings were not always gentle. Hawaiian lāʻau lapaʻau and Māori rongoā were suppressed by missions and by law, and they were later recognized again. South Africa's rooibos (2019) and Hoodia (2003) agreements began to return a share of the benefits to Khoi and San communities.

## 9. New shoots: the modern descendants

In 1790 Hahnemann tested cinchona on himself while translating Cullen, and homeopathy followed. Benedict Lust joined Kneipp's water cure with the name "naturopathy," which he bought from a homeopath in 1902. Osteopathy (1874) grew from frontier bone-setting, and chiropractic (1895) from magnetic healing. In 1910 the Flexner Report made the Johns Hopkins model the standard for all US medical schools, and about a third of the schools closed. This section describes history only. It makes no claim that these methods work.

## 10. Old plants, new drugs

Modern pharmacology is a grafted branch. Variolation, whose exact origin in Asia is unknown (early Indian claims are disputed), passed through the Ottoman world to England in 1721 and led to Jenner's vaccination in 1796. A Shropshire family recipe became Withering's digitalis (1785). Willow bark led to aspirin (1899) and opium to morphine (1804). Andean bark gave quinine (1820). A Jamaican periwinkle tea led to vinblastine (1958). Bundjalung tea-tree knowledge led to Penfold's studies in the 1920s. And in the 1970s Tu Youyou read Ge Hong's 4th-century advice on qinghao, changed her extraction method, and isolated artemisinin, work that won the Nobel Prize in 2015.

One tree, many roots. Every branch still carries sap from the others.
