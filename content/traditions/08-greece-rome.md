# Greek and Roman

*A god struck down for healing too well, a doctor whose name became the label on books mostly written by others, and a pile of manuscripts that crossed three languages and a thousand years to reach your doctor's office.*

---

## The legend

*What follows is how the Greeks told it. It is myth, and it is told here as myth.*

Long before there were doctors in Greece, the stories say, there was a child pulled from fire. His mother, Coronis, had died, and as her body burned, the god Apollo snatched their son from the flames. The boy was named Asclepius (as-KLEE-pee-us).

Apollo carried him to Mount Pelion, a forested mountain in Thessaly, and left him with Chiron. Chiron was a centaur, half man and half horse, and the Greeks remembered him as the wisest teacher of heroes. In the caves and meadows of the mountain, the tale goes, Asclepius learned which roots and leaves could ease pain, how to close a wound, and how to watch a sick person closely enough to understand what was wrong.

He became so skilled that he went too far. In the best-known versions, Asclepius began bringing the dead back to life. Zeus, king of the gods, feared that mortals would escape death altogether and stop being mortal. So he killed Asclepius with a thunderbolt. Furious, Apollo killed the Cyclopes, the one-eyed giants who forged Zeus's thunderbolts, and was punished with a year of service as a herdsman to a mortal king. Some tellings say Asclepius was then raised up as a god; others say he was set among the stars as the constellation Ophiuchus, the serpent-bearer.

The myth gave him a family, and the family gave us words. Two of his daughters were Hygieia (health), whose name survives in "hygiene," and Panacea (all-healing), whose name survives as the word for a cure-all. Asclepius himself is usually shown with a staff and a single snake wrapped around it: the Rod of Asclepius. The ancient storytellers never agreed on the details, not even on who his mother was.

## What the record shows

Legends leave no fingerprints. Stones and texts do.

**The sanctuaries.** The legend of Asclepius grew into real places of healing called *Asclepieia* (singular: *Asclepieion*), temples where the sick came for help. The most famous stood at Epidaurus, in the northeastern Peloponnese. Archaeologists began digging there systematically in 1881, under the Greek archaeologist Panagiotis Kavvadias. In 1988 UNESCO listed the Sanctuary of Asklepios at Epidaurus as a World Heritage Site, in part for its role in spreading healing sanctuaries across the classical world. [3][4]

At the center of the healing was a ritual the Greeks called *enkoimesis*, which historians translate as **incubation**: sleeping in a sacred place in the hope of a healing dream. Pilgrims slept in a long, covered hall called the *Abaton* ("the place not to be entered" by the unprepared), hoping the god would visit them in the night. The cult of Asclepius at Epidaurus is generally dated to the 6th century BCE, and excavation summaries place the first, simpler incubation buildings around the middle of that century; the grand Abaton whose foundations visitors see today is later, from the 4th century BCE. These dates come from site summaries, so read them as approximate. [3] It was not only dreaming. Surgical instruments found at the site, including scalpels and lances, suggest that people there were also treated with drugs and even surgery. [3]

**The writings.** Around the same centuries, on the island of Cos off the coast of what is now Turkey, lived a physician named Hippocrates. Historians usually place his life in the 5th and early 4th centuries BCE, and very little about him can be confirmed. [1]

What survives under his name is the **Hippocratic Corpus**, a collection of about sixty medical writings. Britannica states plainly that most of them were not written by Hippocrates himself. [1] More texts were added to the collection between its first gathering and its first scholarly edition around the beginning of the 2nd century CE. [1] The 1911 edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* counted eighty-seven treatises and accepted only some as genuine. [2] One 19th-century scholar, Franz Zacharias Ermerins, believed he could see the hands of at least nineteen different authors. [5] Today the honest answer is that **we do not know which, if any, were written by Hippocrates.** The works differ in age, style, method and opinion. [5] Even the famous Hippocratic Oath is suspected not to be his. [1]

## Keepers of the knowledge

**Hippocrates of Cos (5th–4th century BCE).** Picture an island in the Aegean, with salt wind and bright stone. The man himself remains a silhouette. The writers gathered under his name looked for natural causes, wrote down what they saw, and tried to predict how an illness would unfold. [1]

**Polybus (5th century BCE).** Tradition, repeated by Galen, calls him Hippocrates' son-in-law and pupil, and a passage in Aristotle's *History of Animals* is the main reason scholars credit him with *On the Nature of Man*, the text that set out the four humors most clearly. [6][7] Even this family tie is disputed: a 2025 article by the Exeter classicist David Leith argues that the son-in-law story was invented in the Hellenistic period, and that if the treatise dates to the mid-5th century BCE, Polybus and his theory may be older than Hippocrates' own working life. [7]

**Herophilus of Chalcedon and Erasistratus of Ceos (first half of the 3rd century BCE).** After Alexander the Great's conquests, the new city of Alexandria in Egypt became a magnet for scholars. There, for a brief window, two Greek physicians did something almost no one in the ancient world did: they opened human bodies to see what was inside. The historian Heinrich von Staden calls them the "first and last ancient scientists to perform systematic dissections of human cadavers." [8] Ancient writers also accused them of something darker: cutting open condemned criminals while still alive. Von Staden judged that "in all probability" this happened; other scholars treat the charge as uncertain. [8][9] Then, abruptly, human dissection stopped after their deaths. Why it ended so suddenly is still debated. [8] Their own books are lost. What we know of their discoveries comes mostly from later writers who quoted them.

**Pedanius Dioscorides (c. 40–c. 90 CE).** Born in Anazarbus, in Cilicia (southern Turkey today), Dioscorides, according to Britannica, served as a surgeon with the armies of the Roman emperor Nero, which let him study plants and minerals across many lands. [10] Imagine him on the march, kneeling by roadsides in foreign provinces, noting the shape of a leaf, the smell of a crushed root, where a plant grew and what local people said it was for. The result was *De Materia Medica* ("On Medical Materials"), written around 77 CE by Britannica's dating (other sources say 50–70 CE). [10] It described nearly 600 plants. [10] It is the only book whose authorship by him is undisputed. [11]

**Galen of Pergamum (129–c. 216 CE).** Galen was born in 129 CE in Pergamum, a wealthy city in western Asia Minor that had its own famous Asclepieion. [12] After years of study abroad he came home, and in 157 CE he became physician to the gladiators kept by the high priest of Asia. [12] Think of that training ground: sand, blood, torn muscle, the wounds of men who fought for a living. It taught him anatomy the hard way.

He went on to Rome, where he won the trust of the emperor Marcus Aurelius and became physician to the young Commodus. [12] He left Rome in 166, around the time a deadly epidemic struck the city, and returned at the emperor's call in about 168. [13] Because human dissection was no longer practiced, Galen dissected animals, many of them. [8] Sources disagree on when he died; Britannica gives about 216 CE, while others suggest around 199. [12] His influence on medicine in Europe lasted from the Middle Ages until the mid-17th century. [12]

**Hunayn ibn Ishaq (809–873 CE).** He was not Greek or Roman, but without him much of Galen might be lost. Hunayn was a Christian scholar of Baghdad who worked in Greek, Syriac and Arabic. In a long letter (the *Risāla*) listing the Galen translations he knew of, he accounted for close to a hundred Syriac versions and dozens of Arabic ones, made by himself and his circle. Exact counts differ between editions and summaries, so the round numbers are the safe ones. [14][19] He belongs at the end of this chapter and the start of the next.

## How it was passed down

Greek medicine did not travel by chant the way the Vedas did. It traveled by **apprenticeship and by the written page**.

In the earliest days, healing knowledge ran in families and guilds. The Hippocratic Oath itself describes a teacher's obligations to a student as if they were family, and it opens by swearing to Apollo, Asclepius, Hygieia and Panacea. [15] The legend and the profession were tied together at the very first line.

Then came the books. Hippocratic texts were written in prose, mostly in the Ionic dialect of Greek, and gathered into a collection that kept growing for centuries. [1] At Alexandria, the great library and its scholars collected, copied and argued over these writings. Galen in turn wrote long commentaries on Hippocratic works, including *On the Nature of Man*. His commentaries gave the four-humor theory much of the prestige it carried for the next fifteen hundred years. [6]

Every copy was made by hand, letter by letter. When a text stopped being copied, it vanished, which is why the books of Herophilus and Erasistratus are lost.

One copy shows how precious this chain was. Around 512–515 CE, in Constantinople, artists and scribes made a lavish illustrated copy of Dioscorides for an imperial princess, Anicia Juliana. It opens with her portrait. [16] We call it the **Vienna Dioscurides**. A thousand years later, in the early 1560s, the Flemish diplomat Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq, envoy of Emperor Ferdinand I, saw it in Istanbul in the hands of the family of Hamon, physician to Sultan Suleiman. The price, a hundred ducats, was too steep for him, so he urged the emperor to buy it. In 1569 it was sold to Emperor Maximilian II and entered the imperial library in Vienna. It now rests in the Austrian National Library. [16][20] UNESCO added it to its Memory of the World Register, a list of documents of worldwide importance; most sources give the year as 1997. [16]

## How it traveled

Greek medicine spread the way Greek itself spread: by conquest, by empire and by translation.

**Across the Mediterranean with Rome.** After Rome absorbed the Greek world, Greek doctors became Rome's doctors. Dioscorides marched with Roman armies. [10] Galen, a Greek from Asia Minor, wrote in Greek for Roman emperors. [12] The language of medicine in the Roman Empire was Greek.

**Into Byzantium.** When the western half of the empire fell, the eastern half, which we call Byzantium, with its capital at Constantinople, kept Greek medicine alive. Its physicians were organizers and editors. Oribasius in the 4th century, then Aëtius, then Paul of Aegina in the 7th century made shorter, more practical versions of Galen's teaching and played down the theory. [17] Paul's seven-book *Medical Compendium* was used as a textbook for centuries. [18] Dates for these men vary between sources, so treat them as approximate. [17][18]

**Into Arabic.** In the 8th and 9th centuries, under the Abbasid caliphs in Baghdad, came one of history's great translation efforts. Texts usually passed from Greek into Syriac, a language of Middle Eastern Christian scholars, and then from Syriac into Arabic. [14] Hunayn ibn Ishaq often did the first step, and his son Ishaq and nephew Hubaysh did the second. [14] Many accounts place Hunayn at the head of the famous "House of Wisdom" in Baghdad. Some historians dispute this, since none of his surviving translations mention it. [14] His Syriac versions are largely lost, so today we read his work mostly in Arabic. [14]

**And back to Europe.** Those Arabic versions, enriched by new learning, were later translated into Latin and fed the medical schools of medieval Europe. That return journey belongs to the next two chapters.

**What it received.** Alexandria stood in Egypt, a land with medical writings far older than any Greek text. How much Greek medicine borrowed from Egypt and its other neighbors is still debated, and this chapter claims no more than the sources show.

## Signature ideas and plants

**The big idea: balance.** The core of Greek medical thinking fits in one sentence a twelve-year-old can hold: *you are healthy when the things inside you are in balance, and sick when they are not.*

The Hippocratic text *On the Nature of Man* named four bodily fluids, the **humors**: blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile. Each was given qualities. Blood was hot and wet, phlegm cold and wet, yellow bile hot and dry, black bile cold and dry. [6] Health meant keeping them in proper balance. [6] Galen developed this into a full system, and it stayed central to European medicine until the Scientific Revolution. [6] Modern medicine no longer accepts it, but our language still carries it: a "phlegmatic" person is calm, a "melancholy" one (from the Greek for "black bile") is gloomy, and a "sanguine" one (from the Latin for "blood") is cheerful.

The tidy chart you may have seen, with four humors matched to four elements, four seasons and four personality types, was built up slowly. Not every Hippocratic writer even counted four humors; some named two or three. *On the Nature of Man* paired its humors with hot, cold, wet and dry and with the seasons. Galen tied those qualities to the four elements of the philosopher Empedocles (earth, air, fire, water) and wrote about bodily "temperaments," and the neat personality types grew out of later readings of his work. [6]

**The second idea: look for natural causes.** Temple healing thrived alongside the Hippocratic writers for centuries, but those writers tried to explain illness through the body, diet, seasons and surroundings.

**The plants, as history.** Dioscorides' herbal described nearly 600 plants. Britannica singles out a few, among them **colchicum** (the autumn crocus), **water hemlock** and **peppermint**. [10] Water hemlock is deadly poisonous: ancient herbals recorded dangerous plants as carefully as gentle ones. Britannica says the book remained the primary text of pharmacology until the end of the 15th century, and elsewhere calls it the leading pharmacological text for some sixteen centuries. [10] These entries are history: a record of what people used and believed, not proof that any of it works.

## Today

At Epidaurus, visitors walk among foundations where pilgrims once lay down to dream, now protected as world heritage. [3][4] The humors are gone from medical science. Revised versions of the Hippocratic Oath are still recited at many medical graduations, and the Rod of Asclepius still marks medical emblems worldwide.

The books endure as objects of wonder. The Vienna Dioscurides sits in a national library, a 1,500-year-old princess's gift, listed among the world's most important documents. [16] And the words of Greek medicine live in almost every clinic: *anatomy*, *diagnosis*, *prognosis*, *therapy*, *hygiene*, *panacea*.

Perhaps the most lasting inheritance is a way of working, not a remedy: look closely, write it down, compare cases, and be honest about what you do not know.

## Threads to other traditions

- **Received from Egypt:** Alexandria, home of the anatomists Herophilus and Erasistratus, was built on Egyptian soil in a land with a much older medical heritage. The extent of direct borrowing is debated.
- **Gave to Rome (1st–2nd c. CE):** Greek physicians, writing in Greek, became the doctors of the Roman Empire. Dioscorides served with Nero's armies, and Galen served the court of Marcus Aurelius. [10][12]
- **Gave to Byzantium (4th–7th c. CE):** Oribasius, Aëtius and Paul of Aegina condensed Galen into practical handbooks. The Vienna Dioscurides was copied in Constantinople around 512–515 CE. [16][17]
- **Gave to the Islamic world (8th–9th c. CE):** Galen, Hippocrates and Dioscorides were translated from Greek through Syriac into Arabic in Abbasid Baghdad, with Hunayn ibn Ishaq (809–873) at the center. [14] This became a foundation of Unani medicine (*Unani* means "Greek").
- **Gave back to Europe (medieval period):** Arabic versions and commentaries were translated into Latin and shaped European medical schools. See the chapters on the Islamic Golden Age and on European monastic medicine.
- **Gave to modern medicine:** the habit of naming natural causes, the case history, the clinical vocabulary, the Oath and the Rod of Asclepius. Humoral theory itself shaped European medicine until the 17th century and was then abandoned. [6][12]
