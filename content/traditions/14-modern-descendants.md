# The modern natural traditions

*In one restless century, a translator, a priest, a frontier doctor and a magnetic healer each claimed to have found a new road to health. The roads they opened are still busy today.*

> **A note before we begin.** This chapter is history. It tells how these movements began, who built them, and how they spread. It does not say that any of them works. Where modern researchers have tested a founding claim, we say what they found.

## The legend

Every movement in this chapter has a founding scene. Its followers tell it again and again, the way older traditions tell of Dhanvantari or Shennong. These scenes are not myths of gods. They are stories about real people on real days. But they have been polished by retelling, and they are best read as the way each tradition remembers its own birth.

**The translator's bitter bark (homeopathy).** In 1790 a German doctor named Samuel Hahnemann sat translating a Scottish textbook of medicines. He did not accept the author's explanation of why cinchona bark (the bark of a South American tree, used for fevers) worked. So, the story goes, he took small doses of the bark himself over several days and watched what happened to his own body. Homeopaths tell this as the moment their system was born.[1][2]

**The bone-setter's grief (osteopathy).** On the American frontier, Andrew Taylor Still lost three of his children in one outbreak of illness, probably spinal meningitis. Osteopaths remember his grief as the reason he turned against the medicine of his day, and they date his new idea to 1874.[3][4]

**The janitor and the "pop" (chiropractic).** On 18 September 1895, in Davenport, Iowa, a man named Harvey Lillard worked as a janitor in the building where D.D. Palmer had his office. In the story, Lillard said that years earlier he had strained his back, felt something "pop," and lost much of his hearing. Palmer pressed on one of his vertebrae, and Lillard reported that his hearing got better. Chiropractors still mark that date as Founder's Day. The accounts disagree on details, such as how long Lillard had been deaf, and the story rests on testimony Palmer himself later printed.[4][5][6]

**The priest and the young emigrant (naturopathy).** A young German named Benedict Lust fell seriously ill with tuberculosis in America and went home to Germany. There, the story says, the Catholic priest Father Sebastian Kneipp treated him with water, and he recovered. Kneipp then sent him back across the Atlantic to spread his methods.[7]

These are the stories the movements tell about themselves. The record that follows is what historians can check.

## What the record shows

Unlike Ayurveda or Chinese medicine, these traditions are young enough to leave a paper trail: patents, school charters, court records and journals. That makes them unusually easy to date, though the sources still disagree on some details.

- **1790 and 1796: homeopathy.** Hahnemann (born 10 April 1755 in Meissen, Saxony) translated William Cullen's *Materia Medica* in 1790. A *materia medica* is a reference book of medicines and their uses. In 1796 he published an *Essay on a New Principle*, which set out his idea from the cinchona work. He followed it with *The Medicine of Experience* (1805), the *Organon of the Rational Art of Healing* (1810), and *The Chronic Diseases* (1828).[1] Sources disagree on when he first used the word "homeopathy" in print. Dates of 1805 and 1807 both appear.[2]
- **1813: the Thomsonian patent.** In New England, Samuel Thomson (1769–1843) took out a U.S. patent in 1813 for his system of botanical medicine. Families then bought the right to use the system for themselves.[8][9]
- **1874 and 1892: osteopathy.** Still dated his principles to 1874. He opened an office on the town square in Kirksville, Missouri, in March 1875, and founded the American School of Osteopathy there in 1892, in a two-room frame building. The school's own history and some histories of manual therapy record its name a little differently.[3][4]
- **1895 and 1897: chiropractic.** Most historians accept 18 September 1895 as the date of the Lillard adjustment. Palmer opened his first school in Davenport in 1897, though some timelines give 1896.[4][5][6]
- **1890s to 1902: naturopathy.** Lust came to the United States in 1892. In 1901 he founded the American School of Naturopathy in New York and organized the Naturopathic Society of America. In 1902 he bought the rights to the word "naturopathy" from John H. Scheel. The school received its charter in 1905.[7] Scheel is usually credited with coining the word, though some reference works give Lust the credit for naming the movement, and sources disagree on when Scheel first used it. Dates of 1895 and simply "before 1900" both appear.[7][10]
- **1910: the Flexner Report.** The Carnegie Foundation published Abraham Flexner's survey of medical schools in the United States and Canada. It changed the course of every movement in this chapter.[11]

## Keepers of the knowledge

**Samuel Hahnemann (1755–1843).** Hahnemann spoke many languages and paid his way through medical school by translating. Just before he earned his medical degree at Erlangen in 1779, he spent two years as personal physician and librarian to Samuel von Brukenthal, the governor in Hermannstadt, now Sibiu in Romania. After a few years practicing in Saxony he lost faith in the medicine of his day. He went back to translating, and the translating led him to his own ideas. He lectured at the University of Leipzig from 1812 to 1821 and practiced in the small town of Cöthen from 1821 to 1835. Then, in 1835, at about eighty, he married his second wife and moved with her to Paris, where he practiced until he died on 2 July 1843.[1]

**Samuel Thomson (1769–1843).** Thomson was a New Hampshire farmer with no medical degree who set himself against the doctors of his day. He built a system around a small number of plant remedies, steam baths and enemas, and he numbered his remedies. Lobelia, a plant that causes vomiting, was his "No. 1."[8][9] His career included a trial in Salem, Massachusetts, in December 1809, after a young patient died and Thomson was charged with killing him with lobelia. Thomson was acquitted. His defense said the powder was marsh rosemary, not lobelia, and the surviving accounts still disagree about what was given.[9] Historians still argue about whether he was a people's reformer or an early patent-medicine seller. The evidence supports both readings.[12]

**Andrew Taylor Still (1828–1917).** Still's father was both a physician and a Methodist minister. Still learned medicine the old way, from books and by working beside his father. He may have had some formal training at a school in Kansas City, but the records do not confirm it. From 1874 he advertised himself as "the Lightning Bone Setter." He opened an office in Kirksville in March 1875 and taught the idea that health depends on free flow in the arteries, which his followers called the "Law of the Artery." He died in Kirksville on 12 December 1917. By then, one history records, about 3,000 Doctors of Osteopathy had graduated.[4] His autobiography was revised several times, and historians now study those revisions to see how he reshaped his own story as osteopathy moved into mainstream health care.[13]

**Daniel David Palmer (1845–1913).** Palmer was born in Canada and worked in a factory there until 1865, when he and his brother rejoined their family in the United States. He spent about twenty years as a horticulturist, schoolteacher and farmer, then became a "magnetic healer." Magnetic healing was a popular belief that a healer could move a vital force through the body with the hands. In 1906 he was prosecuted for practicing medicine without a license. He served 23 days in jail and was fined $350.[4][6] His son B.J. Palmer went on to build the Davenport school into a lasting institution.[4][6]

**Sebastian Kneipp (1821–1897) and Benedict Lust (1872–1945).** Kneipp was a Bavarian Catholic priest known for treatment with water. In American naturopathy he matters most as the teacher who sent Lust abroad.[7] Lust was a tireless organizer. He opened a Water Cure Institute in New York, set up Kneipp Societies around the country, founded a school, bought a name, and in 1919 merged the societies into the American Naturopathic Association. In 1921 it elected him its lifetime president.[7]

## How it was passed down

These traditions were never chanted or memorized in verse. They were spread by a modern toolkit: the printed book, the membership society, the private school, and the license.

Hahnemann's chain ran through his books and through his lectures to students at Leipzig.[1] Thomson's chain ran through a purchase: a family bought the right to use the system, then learned it from his printed guide.[8][12] Folk healing had always lived in households. What was new was a patented, printed system sold straight to families, built to let them do without doctors.

Osteopathy and chiropractic were spread by schools. Kirksville graduated its first class, five women and sixteen men, in 1894, and by 1917 osteopathy had trained thousands.[3][4] Palmer's school had only 15 graduates by 1902, but the family turned it into a training center that still runs today.[4][6] Naturopathy combined all three methods. Lust published, organized societies and ran a school. In the early 1900s, one reference work counts about 20 naturopathic schools in the United States.[7]

## How it traveled

These movements traveled by steamship, railroad and printing press, and so they could cross an ocean within a single lifetime.

Homeopathy left Germany with its founder's books and followed his own path to Paris in 1835. How quickly it reached each country is a story this chapter's sources do not settle. The Kneipp water cure crossed the Atlantic in a single lifetime. It went from a Bavarian parish to New York, where it was renamed and grew into an American profession.[7]

Osteopathy and chiropractic grew up in the American Midwest, a day's journey apart. Some historians have wondered whether Palmer ever met or learned from Still. One history calls it "reasonable to assume" they were in contact, but no record proves it, and the question is still open.[4]

Then came 1910. Flexner measured every medical school against the Johns Hopkins model. That model required a university education before admission, two years of laboratory science, and clinical training in a teaching hospital.[11] In one historian's words, Flexner "sounded the death knell" for the for-profit proprietary medical schools, and about a third of American medical schools closed in the years after the report.[11] Many schools of the natural movements were small and privately owned, so they were exposed to the new standard. Naturopathy declined dramatically in the 1930s, for several reasons.[7] The new standards are often named among them, but Flexner was only one of several causes.

## Signature ideas and plants

The worldviews are easy to state in plain words. Remember that they are beliefs and history, not proven facts.

- **"Like cures like" (homeopathy).** Hahnemann noticed that cinchona produced effects in a healthy person that resembled the illness it was used for. From that he built a general principle: a substance that causes certain symptoms in the healthy could treat similar symptoms in the sick.[1][2] Researchers who have repeated his cinchona experiment under careful conditions have not confirmed what he reported.[14]
- **Heat and the vital fire (Thomsonianism).** Thomson's system tried to restore the body's warmth with steam baths, capsicum peppers and emetics (substances that cause vomiting). He sorted his remedies into six numbered classes, from emetics as No. 1 to "antiseptics" as No. 6.[8][9]
- **The free-flowing artery (osteopathy).** Still taught that the body has its own powers of repair, and that a structural problem blocking the blood interferes with them.[4]
- **The pinched nerve (chiropractic).** Palmer taught that a misaligned vertebra presses on nerves. His followers called this the "Law of the Nerve."[4] The word *chiropractic* comes from Greek words meaning "hand" and "done by." A patient of Palmer's who was a minister, usually named as the Rev. Samuel Weed, is credited with suggesting it.[4][5]
- **Nature's own cure (naturopathy).** Naturopathy began as a drugless approach built on water, diet, air and natural living, with roots in Kneipp's hydrotherapy (treatment with water).[7]

**Plants as history.** One plant connects these new movements to the old traditions: **cinchona**. Its bark came out of the Andes (see chapter 11), entered European medicine books like Cullen's, and set off Hahnemann's experiment.[1] **Lobelia**, a North American plant, became Thomson's "No. 1."[9] We name them as history, not as remedies.

## Today

Every one of these movements survived, though in very different forms.

- **Osteopathy** in the United States moved toward mainstream medicine. Historians read the revisions of Still's autobiography as one step on that road.[13] By 2007 there were about 20 osteopathic colleges enrolling nearly 10,000 students.[4] Still's Kirksville school continues as A.T. Still University.[3]
- **Chiropractic** continues at Palmer College in Davenport and at other schools.[6]
- **Naturopathy** revived from about the late 1970s, after its long decline. As of 2003, one reference work listed twelve U.S. states plus Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands that licensed naturopaths, with schools in places such as Portland, Oregon, Seattle and Toronto.[7] Licensing has changed since then, state by state.
- **Homeopathy** is still practiced worldwide and is still disputed. Rigorous tests of its founding experiment have not confirmed Hahnemann's results.[14]
- **Western herbalism** lives on partly through Thomson's followers and the 19th-century American "Eclectic" doctors, a related but separate botanical movement whose books are still read by today's herbalists. Archives of Eclectic texts still circulate.[9]
- **Integrative medicine** is the modern name for efforts to bring some of these practices into conventional settings. Its institutions and their history deserve their own careful telling and are not detailed here.

## Threads to other traditions

- **Andes → Europe → homeopathy (1790).** Cinchona bark from the Andes, recorded in Cullen's Scottish *Materia Medica*, became the starting point of Hahnemann's 1790 experiment.[1]
- **European academic medicine → homeopathy (1790–1796).** Hahnemann built his system as a protest against the medicine he had trained in and translated.[1]
- **Homeopathy → naturopathy (by 1902).** The word "naturopathy" is usually credited to John H. Scheel, described as a homeopathic physician, and Lust bought the rights to it in 1902.[7][10]
- **Bavarian water cure → American naturopathy (1890s–1919).** Kneipp's hydrotherapy reached New York through Lust and his Kneipp Societies. In 1901 he organized the Naturopathic Society of America, and in 1919 he merged the Kneipp Societies into the American Naturopathic Association.[7]
- **Magnetic healing → chiropractic (1895).** Palmer was a magnetic healer before he founded chiropractic.[4]
- **Bone-setting → osteopathy (1874).** Still presented himself as a "Lightning Bone Setter" before osteopathy had its name.[4]
- **Johns Hopkins model → all of the above (1910).** The Flexner Report made the Hopkins laboratory-and-hospital model the standard that every school was judged against.[11]
- **New England folk botany → Thomsonianism → American herbalism (1813 onward).** Thomson's patented botanical system is remembered by some writers as a root of later American plant medicine.[8][12]
