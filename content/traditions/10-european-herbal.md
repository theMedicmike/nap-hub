# European folk, monastic and herbal medicine

*A king's healing book, a garden drawn on calfskin, a nun who wrote down every herb she knew, and a country doctor who heard about a secret family recipe: the story of how Europe's kitchen gardens became a library.*

## The legend

Europe has no single founding god of medicine. It has stories that people told about where their healing knowledge came from, and some of those stories are better than the facts.

**The gift from Jerusalem.** Inside an English healing book from about 950 is a chapter of remedies that, according to a note in the book, Elias, Patriarch of Jerusalem, sent to Alfred the Great, the Anglo-Saxon king who ruled Wessex in the late ninth century. The note says rare medicines came with them. Picture a messenger crossing half the known world with jars and parchment for an English king. The note is the main reason scholars link the book to Alfred's court, but it is a claim made inside the book itself, and Alfred's role is possible rather than proven. [2]

**The four masters of Salerno.** The southern Italian city of Salerno had Europe's most famous early medical school, and later tradition gave it a perfect origin story: the school was founded by four teachers, a Greek, a Latin, a Jew and an Arab. It is a legend, and no record supports it. It survived because it described something true. Salerno really did sit where Greek, Latin, Jewish and Arabic learning met. [1][13]

**The old woman of Shropshire.** The most famous European herbal legend is quite recent. A wise old village herbalist, "Mother Hutton," is supposed to have handed the secret of foxglove to a doctor. The doctor was real, and he did write that he had been told about an old woman's recipe (see below). But he never named her and never said he met her. "Mother Hutton," with her face and her name, was created in 1928 for a drug company's advertising campaign. A historian of cardiology later called the episode "the making of a myth." [12][17]

## What the record shows

**Before writing.** The Celtic and Germanic peoples of northern Europe left very little writing about their healing. Most of what we know comes from later Christian scribes who wrote down older practices, so the earliest layers are hard to separate from what the scribes added. Historians are careful here, and this chapter is too.

**Bald's Leechbook (about 950).** In Old English a *læce* (leech) was a healer, and a *leechbook* was a healer's book. The most important one survives as a single manuscript in the British Library, Royal MS 12 D XVII, dated to about the middle of the tenth century. Its handwriting points to Winchester, then the royal capital, because the same scribe also wrote the annals for the years 925 to 955 in the Parker Chronicle (a year-by-year record of English history). [2][3][4] A Latin verse at the end gives the book its name. It says that Bald owns the book and that he ordered someone named Cild to compile it. [2] Scholars think the surviving copy was itself made from an older manuscript. [3]

The book mixes three kinds of knowledge. There are remedies adapted from Mediterranean (Greek and Latin) medicine, there are native English plant recipes, and there are charms: spoken or written words meant to work alongside the herbs. [2] A third section, now called *Leechbook III*, follows the closing verse and is not credited to Bald. The scholar M. L. Cameron judged that its 73 recipes reflect everyday Anglo-Saxon practice most closely, with the least borrowing from the Mediterranean. [2]

**The Plan of St Gall (819 or around 827–830).** In a Swiss abbey library lies a sheet of parchment, sewn from five pieces and about 112 by 78 centimeters, that the library calls the oldest surviving architectural drawing of the Western world. It was most likely drawn at the island monastery of Reichenau and was dedicated to Gozbert, abbot of St Gall. It shows around fifty buildings (counts differ, from about 40 to 52, depending on what is counted) and carries 333 Latin labels. [5] It is an ideal monastery, a plan for how monks should live, not a survey of one that was built. Near the infirmary and the physician's house it shows a small walled physic garden (a garden of medicinal plants) with sixteen rectangular beds, each labeled with a plant. Garden historians read sage, fennel, lovage, mint, rosemary, lilies and roses among the names. [6]

**Salerno (from roughly the 800s to the 1100s).** The U.S. National Library of Medicine dates the school's founding to around 800 CE. Other historians see a slower, more gradual start, and the honest answer is that nobody can point to a founding day. [1][13] What is clear is why it grew. Salerno was a busy port linking southern Italy with North Africa, Greece and the Middle East, and its healers could reach Arabic and Byzantine medical books. Its physicians included both men and women. [1]

## Keepers of the knowledge

**Bald and Cild.** We know them only from one line of Latin: Bald, the owner who wanted a book, and Cild, the compiler who made it. Nothing else is known about them. [2]

**Constantine the African (died around 1087, or at least before 1099).** He came from North Africa and in the 1070s joined the great Benedictine abbey of Monte Cassino, near Salerno. There he translated Arabic medical books into Latin. [1][13][18] His *Pantegni* was a Latin version of the *Royal Book* by the Persian physician ʿAli ibn al-ʿAbbas al-Majusi, known in Europe as Haly Abbas. His *Viaticum*, "the book for the journey," was a Latin version of a traveler's handbook of medicine by Ibn al-Jazzar of Kairouan in North Africa. Constantine often did not name the authors he translated, so for centuries some of their work passed under his name. [18] After Constantine, Salerno shifted from bedside tips toward theory, and his translations shaped Europe's first universities. [1]

**Trota of Salerno (12th century).** For centuries Europe believed in "Trotula," a great woman doctor of Salerno. The historian Monica H. Green has shown that the truth is more interesting. *Trotula* was the name of a group of three texts on women's medicine. Twelfth-century Salerno had women named Trota (locally spelled *Trocta*), and one of them was a real healer and writer. The middle text, *On Treatments for Women*, comes from her practice. [7] She is one of the very few medieval women healers whose own work can be traced.

**Hildegard of Bingen (about 1098–1179).** She was a Benedictine abbess in the Rhineland who had visions, composed music, and wrote to popes and emperors. She is also credited with two works on nature and healing. *Physica* runs to nine books, covering plants, elements, trees, stones, fish, birds, animals, reptiles and metals, and it is a practical collection rather than a book of moral allegory. *Causae et curae* ("Causes and Cures") sets out a theory of the body. [8] Both stand in the classical tradition that came down from writers such as Dioscorides and Galen, though she seldom names her sources. [8] Their history comes with a puzzle. *Causae et curae* survives complete in only one thirteenth-century manuscript, found in Copenhagen's Royal Library in 1859, and scholars such as Laurence Moulinier have questioned when and how these works took their present form. [8] In 2012 the Catholic Church named her a Doctor of the Church, a title given to only a handful of women. [9]

**Leonhart Fuchs (De historia stirpium, Basel, 1542).** He was a German physician and botanist who insisted that a plant picture should show the plant itself. His herbal is traditionally regarded as one of the first in which the large woodcut illustrations were drawn from living plants instead of being copied from older books. [10] When William Withering later looked for the earliest description of the foxglove, Fuchs was the author he pointed to. [11]

**John Gerard (The Herball, 1597).** He was a London barber-surgeon and the keeper of a famous garden. His *Herball* began as something else. The publisher John Norton had commissioned a physician, Robert Priest, to translate the Latin herbal of the Flemish botanist Rembert Dodoens into English. Priest died before finishing, and Gerard completed and reworked the text and published it under his own name. Most of its roughly 1,800 woodcuts were printed from blocks Norton brought over from Frankfurt, made for the German herbalist Tabernaemontanus. Historians still argue over how much of the book was Gerard's own. [10][19]

**Nicholas Culpeper (1649 and 1652).** He was a London apothecary (a pharmacist who also advised patients). In 1649 he translated the College of Physicians' official Latin list of medicines, the *Pharmacopoeia Londinensis*, into English and added his own instructions. That put the doctors' recipes into ordinary hands, and it made the College angry. [14] In 1652 he published *The English Physitian*, later expanded as the *Complete Herbal*, which paired cheap local plants with the star-lore of his day. It has stayed in print ever since. [14][15]

**William Withering (born 1741, Wellington, Shropshire).** He was a physician and botanist and a member of Birmingham's Lunar Society. In his book he wrote that in 1775 he was asked his opinion of a family recipe for dropsy, an old word for painful swelling from fluid trapped in the body. He was told it had long been kept secret by an old woman in Shropshire, and it mixed many herbs. Withering judged that the foxglove was the one that mattered. Over about the next ten years he recorded what it did in his own patients, 156 cases by the usual count, including when it harmed them. *An Account of the Foxglove* appeared in 1785. [11][16] Erasmus Darwin, a fellow Lunar Society member, published on foxglove the same year and claimed priority, and the quarrel between the two men became bitter. [16]

## How it was passed down

For most of European history, healing knowledge moved the way family recipes still do: by watching, doing and remembering. Little of it was written down, and much of it is lost.

Writing made a second, slower chain. Monks copied Latin texts descended from Greek medicine, kept herb gardens, and nursed the sick in their infirmaries. The St Gall plan shows that arrangement in ink, with the garden, the physician's house and the infirmary close together. [5][6]

Verse helped people remember. The *Regimen sanitatis Salernitanum*, a rhyming Latin poem of everyday health advice credited to Salerno, was memorized, copied and translated for centuries. English versions stayed in print into the early modern period. [1] Nobody knows who wrote it, and historians usually place it in the 12th or 13th century. [13]

Then came the schools. Salerno, and after it the universities, turned healing into a course of study with set books such as the *Articella*, a teaching anthology built around a Latin translation of an Arabic introduction to Galen by Hunayn ibn Ishaq. [1] Finally, from the mid-1400s the printing press turned one manuscript into a thousand copies. Herbals were among the first big illustrated bestsellers, and the woodcut picture of a single plant could travel from one country's book to another's. [10]

## How it traveled

Europe's herbal tradition was a crossroads more than a homeland.

- **From the Mediterranean north.** Greek and Roman medicine reached England through Latin texts that monks copied and adapted. Bald's Leechbook shows a blending of native and Mediterranean medicine around 950. [2]
- **From Baghdad, by way of Monte Cassino and Toledo.** In Baghdad, the translator Hunayn ibn Ishaq (about 809–873) had turned Greek works into Arabic. Two centuries later Constantine the African turned Arabic works into Latin at Monte Cassino. A second wave of translation began in Toledo, Spain, from around 1100. [1] Greek knowledge came back to Europe enriched by centuries of Persian and Arab physicians. (See chapter 9.)
- **Across the Low Countries, Germany and England by printing.** Fuchs's plant pictures of 1542 were copied or adapted in Bock's German herbal (1546) and Dodoens's Flemish *Cruydeboeck* (1554). Dodoens was put into English by Henry Lyte in 1578, and Dodoens's later Latin herbal became the base of Gerard's *Herball* in 1597. [10] A plant's description could pass through four languages in fifty years.
- **Across the Atlantic.** Culpeper's name went with English settlers. A Boston edition of *The English Physician*, printed in 1708, is often called the first medical book printed in the American colonies, although an earlier one-page Boston broadside on smallpox and measles (1677/78) complicates that "first." [15][20]
- **From the village to the laboratory.** The foxglove passed from a Shropshire household recipe to a published clinical study in 1785. From there, chemists in the next two centuries isolated the digitalis compounds. [11][16] (See chapter 15.)

## Signature ideas and plants

**The worldview.** Learned European medicine was built on the Greek idea of the four humors: blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile. Health meant keeping them in balance through food, sleep, exercise, bleeding and plants. Hildegard placed that body inside a created cosmos where every stone and herb had its nature. [8] Village practice was more practical and more local, and it sat comfortably beside prayers and charms. In Bald's Leechbook, one horse remedy calls for words of blessing to be written on a knife handle, and it suggests the pain may have been caused by an elf. [2] Culpeper added astrology, matching plants to planets. Running through it all was an idea later called the "doctrine of signatures": the belief that a plant's shape or color hinted at its use. This chapter describes these ideas as history. It does not claim that any of them worked.

**Plants as history, never as cures:**

- **Garlic, leek and wine.** In 2015 a team led by the microbiologist Freya Harrison recreated an eye-salve recipe from Bald's Leechbook. It combined garlic, a leek-family plant, wine and ox bile, left to stand in a brass vessel for nine days. In laboratory tests and in a mouse wound model, the mixture killed the bacterium MRSA, though the ingredients did not do so on their own. It was a laboratory finding about a medieval recipe, not evidence of a treatment for people. Its real lesson was that old texts can hold ideas worth testing. [2]
- **Sage, fennel, lovage, mint, rosemary, lily and rose.** These were among the residents of the St Gall physic garden, plants that also had places in the kitchen and in church life. [6]
- **Foxglove (*Digitalis purpurea*).** The tall purple spire of hedgerows and woodland edges was described by Fuchs, mixed into village recipes, and studied by Withering. Withering stressed how dangerous it could be, and it is poisonous. It is the clearest case in this book of a folk plant becoming a modern medicine. [11][16]

## Today

The tradition lives on in several places, each honestly different.

- **In libraries.** Bald's Leechbook and the St Gall plan are now digitized and free to see. [4][5]
- **In print and in popular culture.** Culpeper's herbal is still sold, and Hildegard's writings are widely read again. Her popularity today has grown well beyond what scholars can firmly attribute to her. [8][15]
- **In Western herbalism and naturopathy.** These are the direct descendants, covered in chapter 14.
- **In the pharmacy.** Withering's foxglove led, after long chemical work, to the digitalis medicines that cardiology has used for more than two centuries. A modern annotated edition of his book traces that history from 1785 to 1985. [16]

Traditional use is heritage. It is not proof that a plant works, and these old books are best read as a window into how people cared for each other with what grew around them.

## Threads to other traditions

- **Received from Greek and Roman medicine (chapter 8):** Dioscorides and Galen underpin the monastic texts, Salerno, Hildegard's tradition and the Renaissance herbals. [1][8]
- **Received from the Islamic Golden Age (chapter 9):** Arabic medical books, including Haly Abbas's *Royal Book* and Ibn al-Jazzar's traveler's handbook, were translated into Latin by Constantine the African at Monte Cassino in the late 11th century, and in Toledo from around 1100. [1][18]
- **Received from the Byzantine world:** Byzantine medical works were available to Salerno's healers through its port. [1][13]
- **Exchange with the Jerusalem patriarchate (claimed, late 9th century):** a note in Bald's Leechbook says remedies were sent to King Alfred. [2]
- **Within Europe:** Fuchs (1542) passed to Bock (1546) and Dodoens (1554), then to Lyte (1578) and Gerard (1597), through translation and copied pictures. [10]
- **Given to North America:** an edition under Culpeper's name was printed in Boston in 1708. [15][20]
- **Given to modern scientific medicine (chapter 15):** foxglove went from Shropshire folk practice to Withering's 1785 study to the digitalis medicines. [11][16]
- **Given to Western herbalism and naturopathy (chapter 14):** Culpeper's tradition of English-language herbals written for ordinary readers. [15]
