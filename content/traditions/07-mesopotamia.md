# Mesopotamia

*Long before paper, a healer between two rivers pressed a reed into wet clay and wrote down what to give a sick person. The clay survived. So did the words.*

---

## The legend

Mesopotamia, "the land between the rivers," is the plain of the Tigris and Euphrates, mostly in today's Iraq. It handed down no single founding hero of medicine like Greece's Asclepius or China's Shennong. Its healing story begins with the gods, and with sages believed to have carried wisdom down to people.

**This is tradition, not history.** At the head of Mesopotamian healing stood a goddess. In the city of Isin she was called **Ninisina**, "Lady of Isin." Elsewhere she was known as **Gula** or **Ninkarrak**, and over the centuries these names came to describe one great healing goddess. One of her titles was *azugallatu*, "great healer," a title she shared with her son Damu. Her main temple in Isin was the **É-gal-mah**.

Her animal was the dog. When archaeologists dug at Isin they found something no text had prepared them for. Above and beside a ramp leading up to her temple lay the graves of about three dozen dogs (one count gives 33), and roughly half were puppies. Nobody knows for certain why they were buried there. Scholars have suggested several reasons. Dogs may have stood in for the goddess when people swore oaths. A sickness may have been "passed" to an animal so that a person could be well. The honest answer is that historians are still unsure.

The scholars of Babylon also had a story about where their learning came from. A late tablet from the city of Uruk, written around the 2nd century BCE and known today as the **Uruk List of Kings and Sages**, begins with legendary sages, the *apkallu*, who were said to have lived before the great Flood. Then it moves on to human scholars, the *ummânū*, who served real kings. In this way of thinking, knowledge was handed down in an unbroken chain reaching back to the beginning of the world.

---

## What the record shows

Scribes wrote **cuneiform** ("wedge-shaped" writing) by pressing a cut reed into damp clay. Baked clay, even baked by accident in a burning city, can last thousands of years. That is why historians can read Mesopotamian medicine in its own words.

**The Nippur tablet.** In the Penn Museum in Philadelphia there is a small clay tablet from **Nippur**, an ancient religious city in southern Iraq. Its catalogue number is **CBS 14221**. It is written in **Sumerian**, one of the earliest written languages anywhere, and it holds a short series of prescriptions (about fifteen, in its first editors' count). The Assyriologist Samuel Noah Kramer and the historian of chemistry and pharmacy Martin Levey published it in 1954. They called it the most ancient pharmacopoeia, or book of medicines, then known.

It is spare and strange: it names substances and how to prepare them, but gives no amounts and, in its first editors' reading, does not say which illness each remedy was for. It reads like a practitioner's working notes.

Its date is not settled. The Penn Museum gives **about 2200 BCE**. The Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative places it earlier, in the Early Dynastic period (roughly 2500–2340 BCE), and some writers put it later, around 2100 BCE. Egypt's medical papyri are also very old. That is why careful historians say "among the oldest written medical texts," not "the oldest." What is certain is that more than four thousand years ago, someone in Sumer thought medicines were worth writing down.

**The laws of Hammurabi.** Around the middle of the 18th century BCE, King Hammurabi of Babylon had a long list of laws carved in stone. Several of them, usually numbered **§§215–223**, deal with healers. Some speak of operations done "with a bronze lancet," a small blade.

The fee depended on the patient's rank: saving the life or eye of a man of the highest class earned **10 shekels of silver**, a commoner paid 5, and a slave's owner paid 2. Setting a broken bone earned smaller fees on a similar scale. The penalties were just as blunt. If a high-ranking patient died or lost an eye, the surgeon's hand was to be cut off; if a commoner's slave died, the surgeon had to replace the slave. Translations differ in wording and numbering, and historians debate how literally such laws were enforced. What they show for certain is that healers were paid professionals, held responsible for results.

**The Diagnostic Handbook.** Roughly seven centuries after Hammurabi, a scholar organized the most famous Mesopotamian medical work of all. Its ancient name is **Sakikkū**, written in signs that mean "symptoms." Today it is usually called the **Diagnostic Handbook**. It spread across about **40 tablets**.

Each entry follows the same pattern: *if* the patient shows this sign, *then* this is the cause or the outcome. One sign followed another, with the body read in an orderly way. The book was mostly about diagnosis and prognosis, not treatment. Treatment belonged largely to a separate set of **therapeutic texts**. The Handbook was a guide to noticing, naming and predicting.

Scholarship credits **Esagil-kin-apli** with putting it into its final, standard form; an ancient catalogue of his work names him and places him in the time of King **Adad-apla-iddina** of Babylon, who reigned from about **1067 to 1046 BCE**. That is the source of the "11th century BCE" date. The observations in the book are older. It is a collection gathered and refined over generations, and he was its great editor, not its single inventor.

**The library at Nineveh.** In the 7th century BCE, the Assyrian king **Ashurbanipal** gathered a vast library in his capital, Nineveh, in what is now northern Iraq. In **612 BCE** the city was sacked and the library was smashed. Many of its tablets were baked in the fires, and that helped preserve them. Hundreds of medical tablets and fragments survive from that collection, most of them now in the **British Museum** in London. In 1923 the scholar Reginald Campbell Thompson published hand-drawn copies of many of them, but no translation.

Among them is a work modern scholars call the **Nineveh Medical Encyclopaedia**. It is a structured handbook of **twelve treatises**, ordered by part of the body from head to foot and filling around fifty tablets, that pairs diseases and symptoms with prescriptions. The British Museum describes it as the world's first standardised, structured handbook of therapeutic medicine. One surviving piece, museum number **K.71.b**, is a copy of a chapter of the treatise on stomach problems. An ancient catalogue of medical texts from the city of Assur lists treatise after treatise; by Mark Geller's count, the cuneiform record points to some **ninety** medical works. It is a whole literature, still being pieced together.

## Keepers of the knowledge

**The asû.** The *asû* is usually translated "physician." This was the healer who worked with *materia medica*, the substances of medicine: plants, minerals and animal products. The asû ground them, steeped them in beer or oil, and made them into poultices (warm, moist pastes applied to the skin), salves, drinks and bandages. The Nippur tablet comes from this world of practical preparation. Hammurabi's laws regulated it.

**The āšipu.** The *āšipu* (also called *mašmaššu*) is often translated "exorcist," and that word misleads modern readers. The āšipu was a learned specialist who read the signs of illness and addressed what were believed to be its unseen causes. He used incantations (spoken formulas), rituals and protective amulets. Much of the Diagnostic Handbook belongs to his world.

For a long time historians set these two figures against each other: the "scientific" asû and the "magical" āšipu. Recent scholars have taken that division apart. Both accepted that gods, demons and the dead could cause illness; their roles overlapped, and they appear to have worked side by side. In the second millennium BCE, one study notes, neither was a temple official: they were professionals in their own right.

**Esagil-kin-apli, the editor.** Picture a royal scholar in Babylon in the 11th century BCE, surrounded by old tablets copied and recopied for centuries, each with its own wording and gaps. His achievement was to turn them into one ordered, authoritative work. He is one of very few people in early medicine whose name can be attached to a specific body of work with real confidence. The historian Mark Geller titled a study of him "A Babylonian Hippocrates."

**The family of Bāba-šuma-ibni.** In the Assyrian city of Assur, archaeologists excavated a house (labelled "N4") belonging to a family of *āšipu* healers who were active across **five generations** in the 7th century BCE. More than a thousand texts were found there, a working family library. Some tablets were written by younger family members, which suggests the craft was also taught at home. It is a rare glimpse of medicine as a family trade.

**Ashurbanipal, the collector.** A king, not a healer, but his library is the main reason so much of this medicine survives.

## How it was passed down

Mesopotamian medicine was passed down mainly **in writing, copied by hand inside families and schools**. In that it differs from the long oral traditions told elsewhere in this book.

Spoken words still mattered. The āšipu's incantations were meant to be said aloud, and learning them was part of his training. But from very early on, the knowledge sat on tablets. A student learned to write cuneiform by copying standard texts again and again. A young healer learned medicine much the same way, copying the works of masters under the eye of an *ummânu*, an expert or master scholar.

Many tablets end with a **colophon**, a short note saying who copied the text, from which original and sometimes for whom. These notes let historians trace the chain of copying. They show that the same works were carefully reproduced for many centuries, and they show the family names that run through the profession, as in the house of Bāba-šuma-ibni at Assur.

Kings then gathered family and school copies into great collections such as Ashurbanipal's.

## How it traveled

Mesopotamia sat at a crossroads, and cuneiform was used across the Near East. Even so, tracing where its *medicine* went is one of the liveliest arguments in the history of medicine.

**Toward Greece: a real debate.** The Greek historian Herodotus, writing in the 5th century BCE, told his readers that the Babylonians had no doctors. He said they carried their sick into the public square so that passers-by could offer advice. The cuneiform record shows how wrong he was, and Geller points to the scale of the surviving medical literature to prove it.

How much did Babylonian medicine shape Greek medicine? Geller notes that the two traditions lived in neighboring regions and partly overlapped in time. The Greek healing center of Kos lies only a few kilometers off the coast of Asia Minor. He has suggested that before Hippocrates there was essentially one major system of medicine across the Near East. Another scholar, Markus Asper, links shared features to transmission between the 9th and 7th centuries BCE.

Others are more cautious. Marko Vitas argues that parallels in a Hippocratic treatise point to a shared tradition rather than direct copying. He also notes a deep difference: Hippocratic writers explained illness through the body's internal workings, while Mesopotamian medicine looked more to outside causes. Geller himself points out what Babylon did *not* have. It had no overall medical theory based on diet and regimen, and no counterpart to the surgery described in the Hippocratic writings. The fair summary is that the two traditions were neighbors that probably talked to each other, and historians still disagree about how much was borrowed.

**Into the Talmud.** A clearer thread runs later. The **Babylonian Talmud**, compiled by Jewish scholars living in Mesopotamia, contains medical recipes. Geller has found Akkadian loanwords and word-for-word translations in them, especially in a collection of remedies in the tractate *Gittin*. Akkadian was the main language of Babylonian cuneiform. Geller argues that cuneiform medicine stayed accessible into the **4th century CE** and strongly shaped Talmudic medicine, perhaps by way of Aramaic translations. Other scholars think cuneiform learning had faded earlier, so on that timing his view is a minority one. If he is right, Babylonian remedies outlived the script they were first written in.

**What it received.** Mesopotamia absorbed as much as it gave. Sumerian learning passed to the Akkadian-speaking Babylonians and Assyrians. Its healers worked in two languages for centuries, and Sumerian survived as the learned language of incantations long after people stopped speaking it.

## Signature ideas and plants

**Illness was a sign to be read.** For a Mesopotamian healer, symptoms meant something. A fever, a rash or a fainting spell might show that a god, a demon or a restless ghost was involved. The Diagnostic Handbook often names the cause as the "hand" of a particular god or spirit. Reading the body was close kin to the other great Mesopotamian science, reading omens. Both rested on the same *if… then* logic.

**Observation was organized.** The Handbook works through the body sign by sign and records which signs pointed toward recovery and which toward death: an early, systematic attempt to sort symptoms and predict outcomes, carried out within a religious worldview, not against it.

**Two hands, one cure.** The asû's remedy and the āšipu's ritual were often parts of one treatment. Medicine, religion and scholarship had not yet been pulled apart.

**Plants on the clay.** Ancient plant names are among the hardest words to translate. Many cuneiform plant terms cannot be matched with confidence to any species we know. With that warning, here are some of the ingredients that Samuel Noah Kramer read in the Nippur tablet:

- **Thyme**, **myrtle**, **asafoetida** (a pungent plant resin) and **cassia** (a bark related to cinnamon)
- **Willow** and **fir**
- **Pear**, **fig** and **date**

Alongside them are salt, saltpeter (potassium nitrate), river bitumen, milk, beer, wine, vegetable oil, and animal products such as snakeskin and turtle shell. Later translators have revised several of these readings, and different books give different lists.

These are **history, not prescriptions**: what a healer in Sumer reached for more than four thousand years ago, saying nothing about whether any of it works. Willow returns much later in this book, in the modern chemistry story; the tablet simply shows it was already among a healer's materials.

## Today

Nobody practices Mesopotamian medicine as a living tradition today. The asû and the āšipu faded along with cuneiform, and nobody can trace an unbroken line of healers back to them.

The texts, though, are having a revival. A Wellcome-funded project of the British Museum and University College London, involving Jon Taylor and Irving Finkel of the British Museum and Mark Geller of UCL, is reconstructing the Nineveh Medical Encyclopaedia and making it freely available online. The Nippur tablet is on display in the Penn Museum's Middle East Galleries. Scholars read these texts to understand how one of the earliest literate societies thought about the body, suffering and care, not to find cures.

What Mesopotamia passed on is mostly a set of habits. It wrote medicine down. It organized symptoms carefully. It treated healing as a profession with fees and responsibilities. And it shared ideas with its neighbors in a part of the world where Greek, Jewish and later Islamic medicine all grew.

---

## Threads to other traditions

- **Sumerian → Babylonian and Assyrian (3rd–1st millennium BCE):** Sumerian medical and incantation literature was taken over, translated and expanded by Akkadian-speaking scholars. Sumerian stayed the learned language of ritual.
- **Mesopotamia ↔ Greek medicine (about 9th–5th century BCE, debated):** Neighboring traditions that overlapped in time, linked through Asia Minor. Some scholars (Geller, Asper) see transmission. Others (Vitas) see a shared culture rather than direct borrowing. Not settled.
- **Mesopotamia → Babylonian Talmud (up to about the 4th century CE, debated):** Akkadian loanwords and translated recipes in Talmudic medicine, especially in *Gittin*, possibly by way of Aramaic. Geller's view, which is a minority position on how long cuneiform stayed readable.
- **Mesopotamia ↔ Egypt:** Both traditions are among the oldest written medicine, and their datings are still argued over. This chapter's sources did not document a direct medical exchange between them, so none is claimed here.
