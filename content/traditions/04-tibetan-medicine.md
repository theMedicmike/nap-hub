# Tibetan medicine (Sowa Rigpa)

*A painted tree with 224 leaves, a teaching said to come from the Medicine Buddha, and a book that pulled together what was known about healing across half of Asia.*

## The legend

Tibetan tradition does not say its medicine began with one person. It says the first teacher was a Buddha.

In the opening scene of the great medical classic, the *Four Tantras*, the Medicine Buddha sits at the heart of a healing city. He glows deep blue, and gods, sages and students sit around him to listen. Everything after that is told as his teaching, passed on to his listeners and then to the world. In the late 17th century, when painters in Lhasa set the classic in color, this scene became the first painting of the set: the Medicine Buddha, teaching [2].

A second story is about kings. Tibetan histories say that when Tibet was a young empire, its rulers sent for the best physicians they could find. One version became famous. Three healers came to the Tibetan court: Bharadvāja from India, Hen-weng-hang-de from China, and a physician called Galenos from the West. In the story they shared their knowledge with the court, and Galenos, the healer from the West, is said to have stayed on in Tibet, where later tellings give him a home in Lhasa and a family [1].

Some later tellings set this meeting under King Songtsen Gampo in the 7th century. Other traditional accounts tell of a still larger gathering of physicians from many lands in the 8th century, under King Trisong Detsen. In these stories Tibetan medicine grew out of what the visitors shared.

These are the stories the tradition tells about itself. They say something true about Tibet: high on its plateau, it stood between India, China and the lands to the west, and it listened to all of them. As the next section shows, though, historians read the details very differently.

## What the record shows

**The legend of the three physicians is younger than it sounds.** Historian Ronit Yoeli-Tlalim traced it back. The earliest version she found is in a 16th-century history, *Scholar's Delight*, by Pawo Tsuglag (1504–1566), which names the three healers and calls the western one "Galenos from Khrom", a name usually linked with Byzantium [1]. Later sources, from the 17th to the 19th centuries, place the visit in Songtsen Gampo's 7th century. The 17th-century regent Sangye Gyatso added new details: that Galenos stayed mainly in Lhasa, took a wife and had three sons [1]. In 1979 the historian Christopher Beckwith brought these accounts to scholars' attention. He identified the Chinese physician with the Yellow Emperor and the Indian one with the sage Bharadvāja, and as Yoeli-Tlalim notes, both are mythical figures [1].

Yoeli-Tlalim's verdict is careful. Tibetan medicine does show real traces of Greek-derived (Galenic) ideas, but they are "clearly secondary" to what came from India and China. She concludes that "we really cannot speak of a 'Tibetan adoption of Greek medicine'" in the 7th and 8th centuries. In her reading, the starring role given to Galenos says more about the 16th and 17th centuries, when those histories were written [1].

**The oldest surviving evidence is a sealed cave.** At Dunhuang, an oasis on the Silk Road, a library cave was walled up in the early 11th century, and inside it were Tibetan manuscripts. Only a handful are medical, among them three on moxibustion (warming points on the skin with burning herbs), one on medicinal substances, and two general texts. Because they were sealed away, nobody edited them later. That lets us glimpse Tibetan medicine while it was still taking shape [1]. They describe cautery and bloodletting by what they call "the Turkic method". One remedy calls for "paper from Persia". They use loanwords for theriac (a famous many-ingredient compound medicine of the ancient Mediterranean and Islamic worlds), camphor and saffron, words that point toward Persian and Arabic contact. One thing is missing: the classic system of three bodily forces, which later became the heart of Tibetan medicine, does not appear in its finished form. Wind is treated as the main cause of illness, and bile and phlegm are each mentioned only once [1].

**The *Four Tantras* (Tibetan *Gyushi*).** This is the founding text of Sowa Rigpa, which means "the science of healing". It has 156 chapters in four parts [2]. Most scholars now date it to the 12th century [1]. Tradition and most modern accounts credit it to **Yuthok Yonten Gonpo the Younger**, usually dated 1126–1202 [3]. Its origins were argued over even inside Tibet. In the 16th century, the physician-scholar Zurkharwa Lodro Gyalpo (1509–1579) wrote about the dispute over whether the text truly came from India [4]. Today the arguments run three ways: it is the Buddha's own word, it is Indian, or it is Tibetan [3].

Modern research has moved the conversation forward. In a 2010 Harvard dissertation, the Tibetan scholar Yang Ga compared the *Four Tantras* chapter by chapter with the great Indian classic, the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya* (the "Heart of Medicine"). He showed that large sections also rest on older works written in Tibetan, among them the *Medical Method of the Lunar King*. He also identified three earlier works by Yuthok that served as drafts [5]. So the *Four Tantras* was neither simply a translation nor something that appeared from nothing. It was a Tibetan synthesis.

Its parts can still be traced. Most of its theory of the body, of illness and of medicines is Indian. Pulse reading and moxibustion show Chinese input. Urine examination shows what Yoeli-Tlalim calls Galenic input, meaning ideas descended from Greek medicine [1]. The older *Lunar King* text, dated somewhere between the 8th and the 12th centuries, contains a section on reading urine that closely resembles Ibn Sīnā's *Canon of Medicine*, the great Persian medical encyclopedia [1].

## Keepers of the knowledge

**Yuthok Yonten Gonpo the Younger (1126–1202).** A Tibetan doctor and lay tantric practitioner. Tradition says that at fourteen he set out across Central Tibet in search of teachers, and that a master named Roton Konchok Kyap passed the *Four Tantras* on to him [3]. Later tradition also remembers an earlier Yuthok, "the Elder", placed in the age of the Tibetan kings. How much belongs to each man is something historians are still sorting out [3][6]. Yang Ga's research makes the Younger's role concrete: he drafted, revised and wove older Tibetan writing together with Indian learning [5].

**Zurkharwa Lodro Gyalpo (1509–1579).** The tradition's great editor and critic. In 1546 he was in charge of a printing of the *Four Tantras* from carved wooden blocks, made with the support of a noble house [6]. He also wrote frankly about where the text came from [4]. In his work, Tibetan medicine looked hard at its own history.

**Desi Sangye Gyatso (1653–1705).** He was regent of the government founded by the Fifth Dalai Lama (1617–1682). He was a statesman, and he was also a scholar of medicine. He wrote the *Blue Beryl*, his commentary on the *Four Tantras*, which became a standard guide to it. Then he did something remarkable: he had it painted. Around 1687–1697, artists in Lhasa produced a set of 79 medical paintings [2]. Historians note that this was also politics: the regent and the Fifth Dalai Lama used learned medicine to define and organize knowledge about the body as part of building their state [2].

Picture standing in front of them. Three paintings show medicine as great trees. Nine trunks split into 47 branches, the branches carry 224 leaves, and flowers and fruit hang at the tips. Each leaf is one teaching to memorize, so students could "walk" the tree in their minds [2]. Another painting follows a baby growing in the womb. Another compares the body to a Tibetan house and to a government, with a king, a queen and ministers, each part given its job. One records bone counts based on what the painting presents as direct observation of the dead. The dietetics painting shows farmers and herders going about their day [2]. Further on come pulse reading, the examination of the tongue and urine, hundreds of plants and minerals, and a portrait of the ideal physician's conduct [2].

The set was first shown to students at the Chakpori Medical College in Lhasa, which opened in 1696 on a hill facing the Potala Palace [2].

**Yeshi Donden.** In 1961, two years after the Dalai Lama went into exile, Yeshi Donden opened a small medical center in Dharamsala, India, to care for Tibetan refugees and keep their medicine alive. It became the Men-Tsee-Khang. By 2010 it ran fifty-four branch clinics, and Tibetan medicine clinics of all kinds in India numbered more than eighty [7].

## How it was passed down

Sowa Rigpa was carried in the memory before it was carried on paper. Much of the *Four Tantras* is written in verse, and students learned it by heart. The painted trees were memory tools too, a "mind map" made of branches and leaves, as the Rubin Museum essay puts it [2].

Learning moved from teacher to student. In Yuthok's story as tradition tells it, the text reached him through a master's personal teaching [3]. For centuries many Tibetan doctors, called *amchi* in the Himalayan regions, learned from a teacher, often inside families or monasteries.

After memory and teaching came the printed book. The woodblock edition of 1546 helped fix the text in print [6]. Desi Sangye Gyatso's commentary and paintings gave later generations a standard way to read it [2]. Then came schools: Chakpori in 1696 [2], and the Men-Tsee-Khang in Lhasa, founded in 1916 by the Thirteenth Dalai Lama [8].

The chain survived some hard breaks. Chakpori was destroyed during the fighting in Lhasa in 1959 [8]. Exiles rebuilt the teaching in India from 1961 [7]. Today the paintings themselves keep traveling. Hand-made copies spread across the Himalayas, the Tibetan Plateau, Buryatia and Mongolia, and a complete early 20th-century set made in Lhasa is kept in Ulan-Ude, in Siberia [2].

## How it traveled

**Into Tibet.** Tibet took in learning from several directions. Indian medical learning came with Buddhism. Chinese methods such as pulse reading and moxibustion came from the east [1]. Persian and Arabic goods and words came along the trade routes that carried Tibetan musk westward; musk was already well known in Arabic writing by the 8th century [1]. An early 13th-century Tibetan text by Che rje zhang ston zhig po set medicine within "seven schools" and listed the lands where healing was known, among them India, China, Arabo-Persia and Byzantium [1]. The flow went on for centuries. In 1675 the Fifth Dalai Lama brought to his court an Indian physician named Manaho, known for cataract surgery, who taught a local Tibetan physician [1].

**North to Mongolia and Siberia.** From the 17th century, Tibetan medicine spread across Mongolia along with the Gelug school of Tibetan Buddhism, and for centuries it was the country's learned, institutional medicine [9]. Monasteries ran medical colleges called *manba datsan*, the first founded in 1662. Counts of how many there were before 1937 vary: Stephan Kloos gives more than a hundred, while other Mongolian scholars count more than forty [9]. In Buryatia, now part of Russia, monasteries also founded medical schools on Tibetan models [10]. In Mongolia the late-1930s purges were catastrophic: very few monastery buildings survived 1937–38, and most senior lamas were killed [11]. Revival began after Mongolia's democratic revolution of 1990 [11].

**Across the Himalaya.** In Ladakh, Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh and other Himalayan regions, *amchi* practitioners have long served their communities; in Ladakh, India appointed some forty "government amchi" as early as the 1970s [7].

**What it gave.** Tibet passed on a synthesis: Indian theory, Chinese techniques and western threads, all organized in Tibetan, illustrated and taught in schools. That package became the basis of medicine across Mongolia and Buryatia [9][10].

## Signature ideas and plants

The core idea is balance among **three bodily forces**, sometimes translated as "humors". They are *rlung* (wind, the force of movement), *mkhris pa* (bile, the force of heat) and *bad kan* (phlegm, the force of cohesion and cold) [3][1]. Health is described as these three in harmony. Illness is described as their imbalance, shaped by diet, behavior, season and mind. The dietetics paintings, with farmers and herders at their daily work, show how closely this medicine is tied to ordinary life [2].

Diagnosis is a craft of looking, touching and asking. The doctor reads the pulse at the wrist, examines the urine and studies the tongue [2]. Historians trace two of these skills to particular sources: pulse reading to China, urine reading to Greek-derived learning that reached Tibet through Persian and Arabic intermediaries [1].

The medicines are compounds. Many plants and minerals are combined in one formula, and the *Four Tantras* paintings devote whole panels to identifying them [2]. Some of the substances in the oldest Dunhuang manuscripts carry the history of trade in their names. Camphor and saffron appear under borrowed words, and so does theriac, the famous compound of the Mediterranean and Islamic worlds [1]. These are records of exchange, not proof that anything works. This chapter tells their history, not their effects.

## Today

Sowa Rigpa is a living medicine, practiced on the Tibetan Plateau and across China's Tibetan regions, in Bhutan, Mongolia, Buryatia, India and Nepal, and in clinics around the world.

In September 2010, India formally recognized it as an Indian system of medicine under the name "Sowa Rigpa". The amending act was published on 27 September 2010. India was the fourth country to recognize it, after China, Bhutan and Mongolia [7]. Even the name was a negotiation. "Amchi medicine", the Ladakhi term, was not acceptable to Tibetans; "Tibetan medicine" was ruled out largely because of India's relations with China. At a national seminar in Delhi in February 2004, the parties agreed on the common ground of "Sowa Rigpa", "the science of healing", though Tibetans in exile continued to press for keeping "Tibetan" attached to it [7]. In 2019 India's cabinet approved a National Institute of Sowa Rigpa in Leh, Ladakh [12].

The question of who owns the heritage is still contested. India nominated Sowa-Rigpa for UNESCO's list of intangible cultural heritage, and in December 2019, meeting in Bogotá, the committee referred the nomination back to India [13]. The year before, in 2018, China's nomination of Lum medicinal bathing, a Sowa Rigpa practice, was inscribed on that list [14]. Newspapers reported a quarrel between India and China over the tradition's legacy [15].

Meanwhile Sowa Rigpa medicines are increasingly mass-produced in factories and cottage industries, a long way from the hand-ground compounds of a village *amchi* [7]. Modern scientific study of those medicines is still limited. Their long use is part of the tradition's heritage, and it is not evidence that they work.

## Threads to other traditions

- **From Ayurveda (India) to Tibet, roughly 8th to 12th centuries:** Indian medical learning, especially the *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdaya*, forms the main body of the *Four Tantras*. Yang Ga's research also shows earlier Tibetan works feeding in [1][5].
- **From Chinese medicine to Tibet, before the 12th century:** pulse reading and moxibustion. Moxibustion already fills three of the few early Tibetan medical manuscripts found at Dunhuang [1].
- **From Persian and Islamic medicine to Tibet, from roughly the 8th century, along the trade routes that carried Tibetan musk west:** loanwords for theriac, camphor and saffron. The urine-reading section of the *Lunar King* text closely resembles Ibn Sīnā's *Canon* [1].
- **From Greek (Galenic) medicine, through western intermediaries:** traces in urine diagnosis. The legend of "Galenos" at the Tibetan court comes from 16th-century and later histories [1].
- **From India again, in 1675:** the Indian cataract surgeon Manaho at the Fifth Dalai Lama's court [1].
- **From Tibet to Mongolia and Buryatia, 17th to 20th centuries:** Tibetan medicine was institutionalized with the Gelug monasteries, taught in dozens (by some counts more than a hundred) of *manba datsan*, and illustrated with copies of the Lhasa medical paintings [2][9][10].
- **From Tibet to India and the world, from 1961:** the exile Men-Tsee-Khang. Sowa Rigpa was recognized as an Indian system of medicine in 2010 [7].
