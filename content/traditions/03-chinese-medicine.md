# Traditional Chinese Medicine

*In 1973, archaeologists opened a tomb that had been sealed for more than two thousand years. Inside, written on silk, were medical texts that nobody alive had ever read.*

## The legend

*What follows is how the tradition tells its own beginning. It is legend, not history.*

Long ago, the story goes, there were no doctors and no medicines. People ate what they found and fell ill without knowing why. Then came Shennong, the "Divine Farmer." Tradition remembers him as the one who taught people to farm, and as the first to work out which plants healed and which ones harmed. He did it the hardest way there is: he tasted them. Leaf by leaf and root by root, the legend says, he tried the green world on his own body, sometimes poisoning himself, so that those who came after him would not have to. Later generations put his name on the founding classic of Chinese medicinal substances.

Beside him stands Huangdi, the Yellow Emperor. He is a figure of myth, a ruler from China's earliest age. In the most famous medical book of the tradition he does not give orders. He asks questions. The book is written mostly as a conversation between the emperor and his learned advisers about why people fall ill, how the body mirrors the seasons, and how a ruler, or a physician, keeps things in balance.

Neither man can be placed in the historical record. Historians treat them as what they are: founding figures. They are the faces a civilization gave to knowledge that many unnamed people gathered over centuries. The legends still matter. They tell you what the tradition values most: patient testing, close attention to nature, and the idea that healing begins with good questions.

## What the record shows

The real story is stranger than the legend in one way. Some of its oldest pages were missing until the twentieth century.

**Mawangdui, 168 BCE.** In 1973, at Mawangdui near Changsha in Hunan province, archaeologists excavated a family tomb. Tomb number three had been sealed in 168 BCE. Among its silk manuscripts were medical texts that had been lost for some two thousand years. One was the *Wushi'er bingfang*, the *Recipes for Fifty-Two Ailments*, a mix of drug remedies, practical treatments and ritual healing of its time. Others described the "vessels" (channels) of the feet and hands and how to treat them with moxibustion, the warming of points on the body with burning dried mugwort [4][15]. These were the working notes of a medical world before the great classics took shape.

**The Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic.** The *Huangdi Neijing* is the founding book of Chinese medical theory. Before Mawangdui, scholars dated it anywhere from the Warring States period (475–221 BCE) to the Han dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE). The silk texts changed the debate. Scholars including Donald Harper, Vivienne Lo and Li Jianmin noted that the systematic theory in the *Neijing* is quite different from the rougher ideas in the tomb manuscripts. That suggests the *Neijing* came later [1].

The historian Nathan Sivin concluded that its core texts probably date to the first century BCE. Paul Unschuld argues that the surviving *Suwen* ("Basic Questions") took shape as a compilation in the Eastern Han (25–220 CE) [1]. The honest answer is that the *Neijing* was not written by one person at one moment. It is a gathering of many writings, some of which disagree with each other and some of which comment on others [1].

We do know some firmer dates. The bibliography of the official Han history, the *Hanshu*, completed around 111 CE, lists a *Huangdi Neijing* in eighteen scrolls, beside a lost "Outer Classic." The physician Huangfu Mi (215–282 CE) was the first known writer to say that those eighteen scrolls were the two books we know today, the *Suwen* and the *Lingshu* ("Spiritual Pivot") [1]. A printed edition from 1339, held by the National Library of China, was the edition UNESCO added to the Memory of the World Register in 2011, described in its nomination as the earliest and best-preserved surviving version [2].

**The Divine Farmer's Classic of Materia Medica.** The *Shennong Bencao Jing* is a *materia medica*, a catalogue of medicinal substances. It is said to have held 365 entries, sorted into three grades: superior, middle and inferior [6]. The original is lost. What survives was rebuilt by later scholars from quotations in other books. Most sources place its compilation in the Han era, often the Eastern Han, and treat it as a written gathering of older oral knowledge [6].

## Keepers of the knowledge

**Zhang Zhongjing (c. 150–c. 219 CE): the physician who watched his family die.** At the end of the Eastern Han dynasty, epidemics swept through China. Zhang belonged to a clan of more than two hundred people. Within about ten years, a historian of epidemics reports, two-thirds of them were dead, and seven in ten of those deaths came from what Chinese medicine calls "cold damage" illness [9]. Out of that grief Zhang wrote the *Shanghan zabing lun*, the *Treatise on Cold Damage and Miscellaneous Disorders* [9][10]. It did not survive in one piece. Later editors rebuilt it: first Wang Shuhe in the third century, and then court scholars of the Song dynasty, who issued it in the eleventh century. It came down to us as two books, the *Shanghan lun* and the *Jingui yaolüe* [10][16]. Britannica calls the work a major influence on traditional Chinese medicine [10]. Zhang came to be honored as the "medical sage," and after the Song dynasty, one modern historian writes, reverence for his book hardened into "blind worship of the classic" [9].

**Ge Hong (4th century CE): the alchemist with a handbook for emergencies.** Ge Hong was a Daoist scholar and alchemist. He wrote a practical book small enough to carry: the *Zhouhou beiji fang*, the *Handbook of Prescriptions for Emergencies*. One line in it, written for intermittent fevers, tells the reader to soak a handful of *qinghao* (sweet wormwood) in two *sheng* of water (an old Chinese measure), wring out the juice, and drink it all [5]. That sentence waited about sixteen hundred years for its most famous reader. In the 1970s the Chinese scientist Tu Youyou was trying to extract a useful compound from the plant, and her heated extracts kept failing. Reading Ge Hong, she wondered whether heat was destroying what she was looking for. A low-temperature extraction worked, and it led to the isolation of artemisinin. Tu shared the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for that work [5]. The handbook did not "prove" anything. What it offered was a clue, which modern laboratory science then tested.

**Sun Simiao (Tang dynasty): the King of Medicines.** Later generations called Sun Simiao *Yaowang*, the "King of Medicines." His two huge collections, the *Qianjin yaofang* and the *Qianjin yifang*, take their names from the idea that a human life is worth a thousand pieces of gold. They draw on knowledge from beyond China. Sun included several formulas named after Jīvaka, the physician whom Buddhist texts remember as healer to the Buddha and honor as a "King of Physicians," and he credited at least one teaching to Jīvaka himself [7]. In Sun's pages Chinese, Daoist and Buddhist-Indian medicine sit side by side.

**Li Shizhen (1518–1593): the man who rewrote the herbal.** By the Ming dynasty, Chinese herbals had been copied, expanded and re-edited for well over a thousand years. Li Shizhen set out to gather, sort and correct that inheritance in one work. He finished the first draft of his *Bencao gangmu* (*Compendium of Materia Medica*) in 1578. It has 1,892 entries, and 374 of them are substances he added himself [3]. He did not live to see it finished in print. Carving of the printing blocks in Nanjing was under way around the time of his death, and the edition is usually dated 1596 [3]. The *Compendium* joined the *Neijing* on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2011 [2][3].

## How it was passed down

For most of its history this knowledge moved through three channels at once.

The first was **the family and the master**. UNESCO's description of acupuncture and moxibustion still names the old routes: spoken instruction and demonstration, master-to-disciple teaching, and knowledge passed among members of a clan [8]. A student learned by watching hands on a pulse, smelling dried roots, and hearing the same teaching again and again until it stuck.

The second was **the manuscript**. Mawangdui shows medical knowledge being copied onto silk more than two thousand years ago. The story of the *Shennong Bencao Jing* shows how fragile those copies were. The book survived only because later writers had quoted it, and scholars had to rebuild it from those quotations.

The third was **the edited classic**. Each generation took up the older texts, rearranged them, and commented on them. Huangfu Mi matched old titles to the books in his hands. Wang Shuhe gathered Zhang Zhongjing's scattered writings. Song dynasty editors reconstructed lost works [1][6][10]. Then printing fixed the texts in place: the 1339 woodblock *Neijing* and the Nanjing *Compendium* [2][3]. Today the chain continues in universities, alongside the old master-and-apprentice lineages [8].

## How it traveled

**From India, along Buddhist roads.** Buddhism reached China from India and Central Asia, and Indian medical ideas came with it. Modern scholars group this body of learning under the name "Buddhist medicine." Translated Indian medical works shaped Chinese medical culture, and Tang-era physicians such as Sun Simiao took them in [7].

**To Japan.** The oldest surviving Japanese medical book, the *Ishinpō*, was completed in 984 by the court physician Tamba Yasuyori. Its thirty volumes are built almost entirely from Chinese sources, more than two hundred of them [11]. In a remarkable twist, it preserves passages from many Chinese medical texts that were later lost in China itself [11]. The oldest and most complete copy is a National Treasure of Japan, held by the Tokyo National Museum [11]. Over the centuries Japan grew its own branch of the tradition, which it calls *Kampo*, the "Han method" (chapter 5 tells that story).

**To Korea, and back again.** Korean physicians studied the Chinese classics and then wrote their own. The greatest result was the *Dongui Bogam* ("Treasured Mirror of Eastern Medicine"), completed in 1610 and published in 1613 [12]. It was later published in Japan and in China, where, according to the National Library of Korea, it was printed more than thirty times. It was added to UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2009 [12]. In this case knowledge that had come from China went back to China in Korean form.

**Across East Asia.** Classics such as the *Shanghan lun* became foundations of medicine in Japan and Korea as well. Today medicine descended from these classics has a place in health systems across Asia, including Japan, Korea and Vietnam [13].

## Signature ideas and plants

**The worldview, in one sentence:** health is balance, and illness is balance lost.

Chinese medicine sees the person as a small version of the cosmos. UNESCO's description puts it simply: the body "acts as a small universe connected by channels" [8]. Through those channels flows *qi*, a word usually translated as "vital energy" or "breath," which has no exact English equal. Two complementary forces, *yin* (cool, still, inward) and *yang* (warm, active, outward), must stay in proportion [1]. In the tradition's own terms, the physician's work is to read the body and help it regulate itself again [8].

**Ways of working.** These include acupuncture (fine needles placed at chosen points), moxibustion (warming points with burning mugwort) [8], and herbal formulas built from many ingredients that are meant to act together. UNESCO added acupuncture and moxibustion to its list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010 [8].

**Plants as history (never as cures).**
- **Qinghao (sweet wormwood):** Ge Hong's emergency-handbook plant. Centuries later it gave modern science the clue that led to artemisinin [5].
- **Mugwort (moxa):** the dried leaf burned in moxibustion, a practice already described in the Mawangdui manuscripts [4][15].
- **The 365 of the *Shennong Bencao Jing*:** the founding classic catalogue, which sorted substances into three grades [6].
- **The 1,892 entries of the *Bencao gangmu*:** Li Shizhen's lifetime of correction and addition [3].

**A gift to the world: variolation.** Before vaccines existed, Chinese practitioners developed a way to try to protect people against smallpox. They ground the dried scabs of a smallpox case into powder and blew it into a healthy person's nostril [14]. The earliest written mention usually cited comes from the physician Wan Quan, in a book published in 1549 [14][17]. The scholar Joseph Needham dated the first variolation to between 1567 and 1572 [14]. Stories that the practice goes back to the tenth century, to a holy man from Mount Emei, rest on a book written in 1808, and historians treat them as legend rather than record [17]. One Chinese historian places its beginnings as early as the fifteenth century [9]. However early it began, it is one of the tradition's most remarkable chapters, and the idea later traveled west, where it fed into the story that led to vaccination.

## Today

Chinese medicine is not a museum piece. It is practised widely in China and is also found in Southeast Asia, Europe and the Americas [8]. It is taught both in universities and through the old master-and-disciple lines [8]. Its classics sit on UNESCO's heritage registers [2][8], and its influence runs through health systems across East Asia [13].

It is also argued over, honestly and loudly. Scholars debate what it means to give a living medical practice heritage status, how master-and-disciple teaching fits modern standards, and how its theories relate to modern science. Modern research has tested some remedies and practices, and many claims remain unproven or disputed. The artemisinin story shows the most hopeful version of the relationship: an old text did not prove anything, but it pointed a modern scientist toward a question worth testing. On this site the tradition is told as history and heritage. Traditional use is not evidence that something works.

## Threads to other traditions

- **Received from India (Ayurveda and Buddhist medicine), first millennium CE:** Indian medical works traveled into China with Buddhism. Tang physicians such as Sun Simiao recorded formulas named after Jīvaka [7].
- **Gave to Japan, by 984 CE:** the *Ishinpō*, compiled from more than two hundred Chinese texts, became a foundation of Japanese medicine and, much later, of *Kampo* [11].
- **Gave to Korea, then received back:** Chinese classics shaped Korean medicine. The Korean *Dongui Bogam* (1613) was later printed more than thirty times in China [12].
- **Gave across East Asia:** the *Shanghan lun* and other classics became shared foundations in Japan and Korea, and Chinese medicine reached Vietnam [13].
- **Gave to the world, 16th century:** smallpox variolation by nasal insufflation, recorded in China from 1549 [14][17].
- **Gave to modern scientific medicine, 1970s:** Ge Hong's fourth-century handbook gave Tu Youyou the clue that led to artemisinin (Nobel Prize 2015) [5].
