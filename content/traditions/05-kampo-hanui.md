# Kampo (Japan) and Korean medicine (Hanui)

*One river of Chinese medicine crossed the sea, and on a peninsula and a chain of islands it became something new. Then a country surgeon in Japan put a woman into a deep, painless sleep and operated on her, forty-two years before Boston's famous ether demonstration.*

---

## The legend

*What follows is tradition and myth, told as such.*

In the old Japanese chronicles, the *Kojiki* and the *Nihon shoki*, a god arrives from across the sea. He is tiny. Sukunabikona, the "small lord of renown," comes ashore and joins the great earth god Ōkuninushi to build and settle the land. The two of them are remembered as the first to teach people medicine, healing charms, and the use of hot springs. In one well-loved tale he falls ill and is revived in the hot spring of Dōgo. Today he is honored as a god of medicine at shrines such as Sukunahikona Shrine in Osaka's old medicine-merchant quarter, Dōshōmachi, where since 1780 he has shared the altar with Shennong, the Chinese god of medicine. The two chronicles do not even agree on which god was his parent. That is the nature of a legend: it is less a record than a way of saying *healing came to us from beyond the horizon.*

Korea's founding story has its own healing plants. As it is usually retold, a bear and a tiger beg to become human. They are given a bundle of mugwort and cloves of garlic and told to stay out of the sunlight. The tiger gives up. The bear endures and becomes a woman, and she becomes the mother of Dangun, the legendary founder of the first Korean kingdom. It is not a medical text, but a culture remembers its beginnings through the bitter green smell of mugwort.

Both legends point in the same direction, and on this point the written record agrees: the deep knowledge came over the water.

## What the record shows

The written history begins with Chinese medicine making its way east.

**Through Korea to Japan.** Scholars generally agree that Chinese medicine first reached Japan by way of the Korean peninsula, probably in the 5th or 6th century CE. Japan's own chronicles, the *Kojiki* (712) and the *Nihon shoki* (720), tell of physicians sent from the Korean kingdoms and of medical books arriving with them. Those chronicles were written long after the events they describe, so their exact dates and names are signposts more than timestamps, and some reference works put the real start of the influence in the 7th century, when Japan began sending its own envoys to China. The broad pattern is not in dispute: Korea was the first bridge.

**The *Ishinpō*, 984.** Japan's oldest surviving medical book is the *Ishinpō* ("Essential Methods of Medicine"), presented in 984 by the court physician Tamba no Yasuyori. It fills thirty scrolls, almost all of them excerpts from Chinese medical writings, carefully sorted by topic. It also has an odd, precious afterlife. Many of the Chinese texts it quotes were later lost in China, so for some of them this Japanese anthology is the only surviving witness. A University of Tokyo study argues that the selection is not mere copying: Tamba's choices show a practical, somewhat skeptical mind that favored what worked over grand theory. The *Ishinpō* is now a National Treasure of Japan.

**Korea writes its own books.** The Korean peninsula did more than relay Chinese medicine. In 1433 the Joseon court produced the *Hyangyak Jipseongbang*, a collection devoted to *hyangyak*, "local medicines" grown in Korea. It ran to 85 volumes and, by one Korean scholarly count, described 703 kinds of native medicinal materials. A huge medical encyclopedia, the *Uibang Yuchwi*, compiled in the 1440s and printed in 1477, followed. Then came the masterpiece.

**The *Dongui Bogam*, 1610–1613.** The *Dongui Bogam* (often translated "Mirror of Eastern Medicine," or "Principles and Practice of Eastern Medicine") was compiled by the royal physician Heo Jun on the orders of King Seonjo. It was finished in 1610 and printed in 1613. It fills 25 volumes in five great divisions: internal medicine, external medicine, miscellaneous disorders, medicines, and acupuncture with moxibustion. Copies of the 1613 first edition survive in Korea, where they are designated National Treasures. In 2009 UNESCO inscribed it on the Memory of the World Register, and UNESCO's own *Courier* called it the first medical text to appear on that list.

**Sasang medicine, 1894.** In 1894 the scholar Yi Je-ma completed the *Dongui Suse Bowon* ("Longevity and Life Preservation in Eastern Medicine"). It set out a strikingly original system, which came to be called Sasang constitutional medicine.

## Keepers of the knowledge

**Tamba no Yasuyori (912–995, by one scholarly reckoning).** Picture a Heian-period court physician in Kyoto surrounded by rolled Chinese manuscripts, choosing passage after passage and sewing them into one ordered whole. His book outlived many of the libraries it drew on.

**Tashiro Sanki (1465–1537).** Tradition holds that Tashiro spent about a decade studying in Ming China, usually dated 1487 to 1498. His life story reaches us mainly through later accounts, so the details are best held lightly. He is remembered for coming home with the newer medical theories of China's Jin and Yuan dynasties, and as the founding figure of the *Gosei-ha*, the "school of later developments."

**Yoshimasu Tōdō (1702–1773).** Yoshimasu was a rebel. He had little patience for elaborate theory, and Watanabe and colleagues quote his rule that physicians should rely only on what they "have actually observed by examination of the patient." He refined *fukushin*, careful palpation of the abdomen, as a way of reading the patient, and matched patterns of signs directly to classical formulas. Modern reviews say his practical approach shapes Kampo practice to this day.

**Hanaoka Seishū (1760–1835).** This is the scene that stops people. Hirayama, a village in Kii Province, on 13 October 1804. A sixty-year-old woman named Kan Aiya has a tumor in her breast. Hanaoka gives her a drink he has worked on for roughly twenty years, starting around 1785. Its ingredients include *Chōsen-asagao* (a moonflower, *Datura*) and monkshood (*Aconitum*), plants better known as dangerous poisons. Hanaoka had taken his idea from Chinese accounts of *mafeisan*, the legendary anesthetic of the 2nd-century surgeon Hua Tuo, whose formula was lost. She sinks into a deep sleep. He removes the tumor.

Many historians of anesthesia regard this as the first reliably documented operation under general anesthesia (a "first" always worth holding a little loosely), 42 years before William Morton's public ether demonstration in 1846. The drink is known as *tsūsensan* or *mafutsusan*, and records credit Hanaoka with more than 150 breast operations using it. The story has hard edges. Kan Aiya's illness had already spread, and she is reported to have died months later. The documents need care too: a 2000 study found forgeries in a 1923 biography's transcription of Hanaoka's case record, and the surviving manuscript may be a disciple's copy. In 1839 his student Kamada Gendai dictated a short booklet on the anesthetic, the *Mafutsutō-ron*, which some historians call the world's first anesthesia textbook. The core facts hold: the year, the patient, and an herbal anesthetic built from Chinese learning by a surgeon who also knew Dutch methods.

**Heo Jun (1539 or 1546 to 1615).** Sources disagree on his birth year. His mother was a concubine, so Heo Jun was born outside the aristocracy into the *chungin*, the "middle people" of Joseon society, and he lived with the discrimination that came with that. He entered the Royal Infirmary, treated King Seonjo, and in 1590 treated the crown prince for smallpox. When Japanese armies invaded in the Imjin War (1592–1598) and other court physicians fled, Heo Jun stayed with the king. The war had destroyed books and scattered knowledge, and Seonjo charged him with writing a new medical work to replace it. After the king died in 1608, Heo Jun was blamed and exiled to Uiju in the far north. He kept writing. Restored to court in 1609, he finished the great book in 1610 after roughly fifteen years of work (UNESCO's *Courier* says sixteen; the count depends on where you start). He deliberately wrote the names of herbs in plain Korean, and he favored cheap, home-grown medicines over rare imports so that ordinary people could use what he described. He also wrote shorter guides in Korean on emergencies, childbirth, epidemics, and smallpox.

**Yi Je-ma (1837–1900; some sources say 1838).** Yi was a late-Joseon scholar and a devout Neo-Confucian, and he asked a question the old texts had not quite asked. Why does the same illness behave differently in different people? His answer was that each person is born with one of four constitutions. He borrowed their names from the *I Ching*, the ancient "Book of Changes": Greater Yang (*Taeyang*), Lesser Yang (*Soyang*), Greater Yin (*Taeeum*), and Lesser Yin (*Soeum*). He tied body and mind together through four Confucian virtues: benevolence, rightness, propriety, and wisdom. One Korean scholar points out that Yi never called his system "Sasang medicine." That name came later.

## How it was passed down

Unlike traditions that lived for centuries in chant and memory, East Asian medicine arrived in Japan and Korea **already written**, carried in Chinese characters, the shared scholarly script of the region. The generational chain was a chain of books, and of the people who read them aloud to students.

- **Court lineages.** In Heian Japan, medicine was kept by hereditary court families such as Tamba's. Knowledge passed from father to son along with the manuscripts.
- **Anthologies as lifeboats.** Compilers like Tamba and Heo Jun gathered scattered texts into single works, and in doing so preserved them. In the case of the *Ishinpō*, the anthology outlasted its sources.
- **Schools and masters.** In Edo Japan (1600–1868), medicine became a matter of rival schools, each built around a teacher and his disciples. The *Gosei-ha* followed later Chinese theory. The *Kohō-ha*, the "school of ancient methods," rose in the late 17th century and went back to the old classic *Shanghan lun* ("Treatise on Cold Damage"). Watanabe and colleagues write that in this period Japanese practice narrowed thousands of Chinese drugs to about 300, combined into about 300 prescriptions, a distinctly Japanese simplification.
- **Royal printing.** Korea's government printed the *Dongui Bogam* in 1613, which put identical copies into many hands, and the book was reprinted again and again.

## How it traveled

The direction of travel reversed more than once.

**China to Korea to Japan (5th–7th centuries).** Medical books and healers moved along the same diplomatic and Buddhist routes that carried scripture, writing, and art across the Korea Strait.

**Japanese scholars to China and back (15th–16th centuries).** Tashiro Sanki's remembered years in China show the traffic running both ways: Japanese physicians went to the source and brought home the latest ideas.

**Korea to the whole region (17th–18th centuries onward).** The *Dongui Bogam* did not stay home. A modern medical-history review reports that it was reprinted and translated in Japan, China, and Vietnam. A Korean book about "Eastern medicine" went on to teach the lands its knowledge had first come from.

**Europe arrives through a Dutch window (18th century).** During the Edo period, Japan's main channel to Western science was the small Dutch trading post at Nagasaki, and the study it fed was called *Rangaku*, "Dutch learning." In 1774 Sugita Genpaku and his colleagues published the *Kaitai Shinsho* ("New Book of Anatomy"), translated from a Dutch version of a German anatomy text. Their first edition had errors and omissions, and it was still a landmark. Hanaoka shows what that meeting could produce: Chinese herbal learning and Dutch-influenced surgery combined in a single operation.

**The tide turns (19th century).** By the end of the Tokugawa period, Dutch and then other Western medicine had become mainstream in Japan. In 1876 the new Meiji government required all physicians to study Western medicine. Kampo was not forbidden, but it was squeezed hard and declined for decades. Korea, too, later built a modern medical system beside its traditional one.

## Signature ideas and plants

*This is history and belief, not medical advice. Traditional use is never evidence that something works.*

**The worldview in one sentence:** health is balance, and the body is a small universe that mirrors the large one.

- **Body as cosmos.** The *Dongui Bogam* opens with cosmology and compares the human body to heaven and earth. In its picture, the pulse carries "celestial energy." Heo Jun believed illness came from imbalance, and the UNESCO *Courier* notes that he ranked tending the mind, and meditation, above medicines.
- **Prevention first.** Heo Jun's book puts care of life and prevention at the center rather than at the margins.
- **Formula matched to pattern.** Kampo's signature, shaped by Yoshimasu Tōdō, is to observe the patient closely (including the abdomen, through *fukushin*) and match the overall pattern to a fixed, time-honored combination of herbs, rather than to a single remedy.
- **The constitution decides.** Yi Je-ma's Sasang idea is that the same complaint should be approached differently depending on which of the four constitutions a person has.
- **Local plants, local pride.** The *hyangyak* movement and Heo Jun's plain-Korean herb names were a declaration that Korea's own hills and fields held medicine worth recording.

**Plants as history:**
- **Mugwort.** It appears in Korea's founding legend, and its dried leaf is the traditional fuel of moxibustion, the warming technique that the *Dongui Bogam* treats alongside acupuncture.
- **Moonflower (*Datura*) and monkshood (*Aconitum*).** These are deadly plants. In Hanaoka's hands, and in carefully measured proportions, they became the basis of an anesthetic. They belong to the history of surgery, not to anyone's kitchen.

The *Dongui Bogam* also has a section of "diverse remedies," which includes, of all things, a preparation said to make you invisible. UNESCO's *Courier* suggests reading it as a lesson in "the way of the heart" rather than a recipe. Even a great encyclopedia carries traces of the marvelous.

## Today

**Japan.** Kampo returned after the Second World War, when the first modern Kampo specialists picked up the threads of the Edo schools. Kampo formulas have been on Japan's national health insurance drug list since 1971, and a review by Watanabe and colleagues counted 148 covered prescriptions. Most are dispensed as standardized extract granules rather than raw herbs. In 2001 Kampo was added to the model core curriculum for Japanese medical students. A survey cited in that review found that more than 70 percent of physicians said they prescribe Kampo. Japan is unusual in that Kampo lives inside mainstream medicine, prescribed by ordinary licensed physicians, while researchers there say plainly that its evidence base is still being built.

**Korea.** Since 1951, South Korea has run two independent, parallel medical professions, Western medicine and Korean medicine (*Hanui*), within one national health system, an arrangement that is rare in the world. Korean medicine doctors train in their own universities and hold their own licenses. Korea's official national disease classification includes a Korean-medicine section with its own codes, among them entries for Yi Je-ma's four-constitution medicine. The government has invested heavily in research. The arrangement is also openly debated. When the *Dongui Bogam* was honored by UNESCO in 2009, the Korean Medical Association, representing Western-trained physicians, argued publicly that the honor recognized a historical document, not the science of traditional medicine. Both views are part of the honest picture: the *Dongui Bogam* is a treasure of world heritage, and whether particular traditional practices work is a question for evidence.

## Threads to other traditions

- **From Chinese medicine (5th–7th c. onward):** the foundational texts, theory, materia medica, acupuncture, and moxibustion, carried to Korea and on to Japan, largely along Buddhist and diplomatic routes.
- **Korea to Japan (5th–6th c.):** Korea served as the bridge, with physicians and books crossing the strait, as Japan's 8th-century chronicles remember it.
- **Japan preserving China (984):** the *Ishinpō* kept the text of Chinese medical works that were later lost in China.
- **China to Japan again (15th–17th c.):** Tashiro Sanki is credited with bringing Jin–Yuan theory home (*Gosei-ha*). Japan's *Kohō-ha* then turned back to a much older Chinese classic, the *Shanghan lun*.
- **Korea back to China, Japan, and Vietnam (17th c. onward):** the *Dongui Bogam* was reprinted and translated across East Asia.
- **Chinese legend into Japanese surgery (1785–1804):** Hua Tuo's lost *mafeisan* inspired Hanaoka's *mafutsusan*.
- **Europe into Japan (18th c.):** *Rangaku* brought Dutch anatomy (the 1774 *Kaitai Shinsho*) and surgery into contact with Kampo, and Hanaoka combined both.
- **Into the history of anesthesia (1804):** Hanaoka's operation stands in the world history of anesthesia, decades before ether, though Western anesthesia developed separately and did not build on his method.
- **Chinese philosophy into Korean innovation (1894):** the *I Ching* and Neo-Confucian ethics shaped Yi Je-ma's Sasang constitutional medicine.
