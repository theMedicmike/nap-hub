# African traditional medicine

*Not one tradition but hundreds, carried in memory, in parchment and in the hands of healers across a whole continent, and still alive in the morning market today.*

---

## The legend

*What follows is tradition. It is how communities speak of their own healing, not a historical record.*

No one legend covers Africa, because Africa never had one medicine. It is a continent of more than fifty countries and a great many peoples and languages, and each healing tradition tells its own story about where knowledge comes from.

Some threads run through many of these stories. Knowledge is often said to come from the ancestors, from the land, and from the plants themselves. The healer is a go-between: someone who listens and remembers, and who owes the knowledge back to the people it came from.

Among the Yoruba of West Africa, tradition holds that the leaves of the forest belong to Ọ̀sanyìn, the *orisha* (divine being) of herbs and healing. An herbalist who goes out to gather plants is working in a world that the tradition sees as alive and spoken for. Scholars who study Yoruba medicine note that it is often hard to say where medicine ends and religion begins [7]. To the people who practice it, the two were never separate things.

That is as far as this chapter goes into the sacred. Much of what African healers know was never meant for outsiders, and it stays with them.

---

## What the record shows

The oldest written medicine in the world includes African medicine. **Ancient Egypt sat on the African continent**, beside the Nile, and its medical papyri (told in full in chapter six) belong to Africa's story as much as to the Mediterranean's. Histories that treat Egypt as if it floated somewhere apart from Africa get the map wrong.

Away from the Nile, though, most African healing knowledge was not written down. It lived in speech, song, apprenticeship and practice. That makes the written record patchy, and dating hard. Many of the plants and practices are likely far older than any document that mentions them. The documents are simply where outsiders first noticed them.

Three bodies of evidence stand out.

**Ethiopia's parchment.** Ethiopia had something rare south of the Sahara: its own ancient written language, **Ge'ez**, used for centuries on parchment, mostly for Christian and Biblical writing [6]. Medicine entered that written world too. One Addis Ababa University study notes that the earliest Ethiopian medical texts known today date to around the seventeenth or eighteenth century [6]. Historians are careful here. Medical books were often kept by the individual healers who used them, not by libraries, so old copies wore out, and the knowledge in them may well be older than the oldest copy that survives. Among the great collections abroad is the Wellcome Collection in London, whose Ethiopian manuscripts the scholar Stefan Strelcyn catalogued in 1972. He judged that many of its protective healing scrolls date from the eighteenth to the early twentieth centuries, and noted that they sit somewhere between medicine and prayer rather than being medical books in the narrow sense [8].

**Timbuktu's libraries.** In the Sahel, in cities like Timbuktu in present-day Mali, families kept private libraries of manuscripts in Arabic and local languages. They were written and copied over several centuries, with some traced back as far as the thirteenth, and they cover the Qur'an, law, astronomy, mathematics and *tibb*, the Arabic word for medicine [10]. Most have never been fully studied or catalogued, so scholars are wary of big claims about what they contain [10]. During the conflict of 2012–2013, some four thousand manuscripts held at the state-run Ahmed Baba Institute were burned or stolen, while hundreds of thousands from the private collections were smuggled south to the capital, Bamako, for safekeeping [10].

**The travelers' notebooks of the Cape.** At Africa's southern tip, European visitors began writing down what the Khoekhoe (Khoikhoi) and San peoples knew about plants. Those notes are not the start of that knowledge. A modern review of buchu observes that Khoisan knowledge of the plant precedes written records, "probably by centuries" [3].

## Keepers of the knowledge

Africa's healing knowledge has mostly been held by people whose names were never written down: generations of herbalists, birth attendants, bonesetters and diviners. Some figures stand out, though, because the record caught them at a turning point.

**The unnamed Khoekhoe and San plant experts of the Cape.** In 1719 the writer Peter Kolb recorded the name "buchu" and described the dried, powdered leaves being used for aches and for anointing the body [3]. Between 1826 and 1831 the botanist Christian Ecklon published a series of articles on the Cape's medicinal plants, buchu among them [3]. The Europeans signed those accounts. The knowledge in them belonged to the people they were watching.

**The debtera of Ethiopia.** In the Ethiopian highlands, a *debtera* is a learned man trained in the church schools, skilled in Ge'ez, chant and writing. A historical overview of Ethiopian medicine lists the debtera alongside bonesetters, birth attendants, tooth-extractors and herbalists among the country's traditional healers [9]. Because they could write, debtera became copyists and keepers of the medical books.

**The keepers of Timbuktu.** The libraries survived because families guarded them across the generations. One of those heirs, the librarian Abdel Kader Haidara, later led the organization SAVAMA-DCI, set up to protect and restore the manuscripts, and was among the chief organizers of their rescue in 2012–2013 [10].

**Dr. Oku Ampofo (Ghana).** Ampofo (1908–1998) was a Ghanaian physician and sculptor. In the early 1960s President Kwame Nkrumah sent him and others to China to study the Chinese experience with herbal medicine [5]. In 1971 Ampofo put a proposal to the government for a national centre to study Ghana's medicinal plants. His team included the Ghana Academy of Arts and Sciences and the Ghana Psychic and Traditional Healers Association, so healers had a voice from the beginning [5]. The centre's own history says it grew from a modest outpatient clinic at Mampong-Akwapim run under Ampofo's guidance. The government formally established it in 1975, and it is known today as the Centre for Plant Medicine Research [5].

**The San and Khoi councils.** In our own time, the keepers of knowledge have also become its legal defenders. The South African San Council and the National Khoi & San Council have negotiated with governments, researchers and whole industries, and won recognition (see *Today*).

## How it was passed down

For most of African history, the library was a person.

An apprentice learned at a healer's side for years. They walked the bush, learned to tell one leaf from its look-alike, and learned when and how a plant should be gathered and prepared. A study of Yoruba herbal medicine describes this knowledge being handed on through a system of apprenticeship [11]. Words carried it too. Yoruba healing traditions are known for their rich language of praise, proverb and incantation, a kind of spoken memory [7].

In Ethiopia the chain had two strands. One was oral, from teacher to student. The other was written, in Ge'ez and Amharic books copied by hand. A 2020 study compared the herbal uses written in one Ethiopian medical manuscript, the *ʾƎṣä Däbdabbe*, with what living traditional practitioners told the researcher. It found the two strongly similar [12]. Put plainly, the knowledge on the parchment was still alive in people's memory generations later.

In the Sahel, the chain ran through families and scholars who copied, annotated and guarded Arabic manuscripts, sometimes hiding them in chests and mud-walled rooms [10].

The fragility of this chain is real. When an elder healer dies without an apprentice, a whole library can close for good.

## How it traveled

African healing knowledge has always moved, along trade routes, with conquests and migrations, and through the forced crossings of the Atlantic slave trade.

**Across the Sahara.** Caravans carried salt, gold, books and scholars between West Africa and the Islamic world to the north. That is how Arabic medical learning, *tibb*, came to sit on the shelves of Timbuktu beside local knowledge [10].

**Across the Atlantic.** Enslaved Yoruba people carried their knowledge of leaves and healing to the Americas. Scholars trace its living echoes in Brazil's Candomblé religion, where Yoruba ways of healing and plant knowledge continue [7].

**Out of the Cape, into the pharmacy.** Buchu is the clearest case of an African remedy entering Western medicine. Joseph Mackrill is usually credited with bringing it to Britain around 1815 [3]. It was being sold into Germany by around 1825, and in 1826 it found a place in the Dublin Pharmacopoeia [3]. (A *pharmacopoeia* is an official list of medicines.) In America it was described in the first edition of the *Dispensatory of the United States*, in 1833. In 1846 an American druggist, Henry T. Helmbold, opened the business that would sell "Helmbold's Extract Buchu", and by 1865, according to the review, it was the best-selling patent medicine on the US market [3]. Buchu exports from the Cape peaked in 1873 at some 400,000 pounds of leaves [3]. Then it faded. The United States Pharmacopeia dropped it in 1930, and in 1949 a British reference listed it as superseded by sulfonamides and penicillin [3].

**From China to Ghana.** Influence flowed into Africa as well as out of it. Ampofo's 1960s study trip to China helped shape the Ghanaian centre that brought traditional healers and laboratory scientists together [5].

**From a book to a patent office.** In the 1930s a Dutch anthropologist published an account of how the San used the Kalahari succulent *Hoodia gordonii*. That knowledge later reached South African government scientists, with consequences told below [4].

## Signature ideas and plants

**The worldview, in plain English.** Across many African traditions, health has meant more than a body without pain. It has meant balance: between a person and their family, their community, their ancestors and the land. Illness could be read as something wrong in any of those relationships, so healing could mean herbs, or setting a bone, or restoring a relationship, or all three at once [7, 9]. Many traditions also had specialists, much as hospitals do. South African law today recognizes four kinds of traditional health practitioner: the **diviner** (in isiZulu often called *isangoma*), the **herbalist** (*inyanga*), the **traditional birth attendant** and the **traditional surgeon** [13].

The plants below are history and heritage. Their traditional use is not evidence that they work for anything.

- **Buchu** (*Agathosma betulina* and *A. crenulata*). These are small, fragrant shrubs of the Western Cape, in the citrus family (Rutaceae) [3]. For the Khoekhoe and San, aromatic buchu plants were used in dance, for anointing and adornment, as perfume and as medicine [3]. Europeans spelled the name more than a dozen ways (boegoe, boggoa, bucco, buku and more), each one an attempt to write down a Khoekhoe word [3].

- **Rooibos** (*Aspalathus linearis*), "red bush" in Afrikaans. It grows wild in and around the Cederberg mountains of the Western Cape [2, 14]. The Swedish botanist Carl Thunberg, travelling the Cape in 1772, left what is generally taken as the earliest written note of the plant being made into tea [2]. How much older that custom is remains debated: the written record from before colonial times is thin, and some researchers argue it says little either way [18]. The Khoi and San communities' own claim to the knowledge was formally recognized in 2019 (see *Today*). In 1904 a Russian immigrant, Benjamin Ginsberg, began trading this "mountain tea", and the South African Rooibos Council credits him as its first exporter [2]. Commercial farming began in the 1930s, encouraged by the doctor and naturalist Pieter le Fras Nortier [2].

- **Hoodia** (*Hoodia gordonii*), a spiny, leafless succulent of the Kalahari. San hunters, according to the accounts recorded about them, chewed it to stave off hunger and thirst on long journeys over hard country [4].

- **Ethiopia's healing herbs.** Ethiopian manuscripts and living practitioners share a large body of plant knowledge [12]. Scholars of Ethiopian medicine warn that this knowledge, and the plants themselves, are now under pressure as wild species disappear [9].

## Today

African traditional medicine is not a museum piece. It is practiced every day, in villages and cities, often side by side with hospitals.

**Recognized in law.** South Africa's **Traditional Health Practitioners Act of 2007** created a council to register traditional healers and set standards for training and practice [13]. Putting it into effect has been slow: draft regulations were published for comment in November 2015, and a new set was put out for comment in 2024 [13]. Healers interviewed by researchers saw registration as a door into mainstream health care and a way to separate genuine practitioners from charlatans. Critics argue the law worries more about protecting the public from healers than about protecting healers from being overshadowed by Western medicine [13].

**Studied in the laboratory.** Ghana's Centre for Plant Medicine Research, founded in 1975 and renamed by an act of Parliament in 2011, still works where traditional plant knowledge meets scientific testing [5].

**Defended at the negotiating table.** This is where African communities have helped shape a newer idea for the world: that traditional knowledge holders should share in the profits made from their knowledge.

*The Hoodia case.* South Africa's Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) had studied hoodia since 1963. It isolated a compound it called P57, filed for patents from 1997 onward, and licensed it for drug development. The British firm Phytopharm, the drug company Pfizer and later Unilever were all involved at different stages [4]. The San had not been told. Advocates said this broke international principles of prior informed consent [4]. In a February 2002 memorandum of understanding, the CSIR formally acknowledged the San people's role. In March 2003 it signed a benefit-sharing agreement with the South African San Council, at Andriesvale in the Kalahari. The San would receive 8% of milestone payments and 6% of royalties that the CSIR received [4, 15]. A San Hoodia Benefit Sharing Trust was set up and first met in April 2005 [4]. The outcome was mixed. Unilever withdrew in 2008, no drug from the research reached the market, and by 2010, critics noted, the San had received only around US$100,000 [16]. But the principle had been established.

*The Rooibos agreement.* In 2010 the South African San Council wrote to the environment minister asking for benefit-sharing negotiations over rooibos, and the National Khoi & San Council later joined it [1]. A study commissioned by the government's environment department and launched in 2014 supported the San and Khoi claims to the traditional knowledge [17]. After nine years of talks, the agreement was concluded on 25 May 2019 and launched on 1 November 2019 [1]. It recognizes the Khoikhoi and San as "the traditional knowledge holders to the uses of Rooibos" [1]. The industry pays a levy of 1.5% of the farm-gate price (the price processors pay farmers for the harvested crop) into two community trusts [17]. Natural Justice, the legal organization that supported the communities, calls it the world's first industry-wide benefit-sharing agreement [1]. The first payout, announced in July 2022, came to about R12.2 million, shared equally between the two councils' trusts [17]. Its legal roots run through South Africa's Biodiversity Act and the international Nagoya Protocol, a treaty on sharing the benefits of nature's genetic resources fairly [1].

Today, when someone in Tokyo or Berlin drinks a cup of rooibos, a small part of what the industry earns goes back to the communities recognized as the plant's first knowledge holders. Stanley Peterson of the National Khoi & San Council put it simply: "Our people are happy and our land healed." [1]

## Threads to other traditions

- **Ancient Egypt to the Greek world:** Egypt's medicine is African medicine. Its exchange with Greece and Rome is told in chapters six and eight.
- **Islamic medicine (*tibb*) to West Africa (over several centuries, from about the 13th onward):** Arabic medical learning reached the libraries of Timbuktu along trans-Saharan trade and scholarly networks [10].
- **Ethiopian Christian scholarship to Ethiopian medicine (surviving texts from about the 17th–18th century onward):** Ge'ez and Amharic manuscripts copied and kept by church-trained debtera [6, 9].
- **Yoruba healing to Brazil (era of the Atlantic slave trade):** Yoruba plant knowledge and healing practice survive in Candomblé [7].
- **Khoekhoe and San knowledge to European medicine (1719–1930s):** buchu was recorded by Kolb (1719), introduced to Britain around 1815, listed in the Dublin Pharmacopoeia (1826) and the US *Dispensatory* (1833), and became a best-selling American patent medicine by 1865 [3].
- **Khoi and San knowledge to the world's teacups (1772–today):** rooibos tea was first noted in writing by Thunberg (1772), exported from 1904 and farmed from the 1930s. Benefit-sharing followed in 2019 [1, 2].
- **San knowledge to modern pharmaceutical research (1930s–2003):** hoodia passed from a 1930s anthropology book to CSIR research (from 1963), patent filings (from 1997) and drug-company licensing, then to the 2003 benefit-sharing agreement [4, 15].
- **Chinese medicine to Ghana (early 1960s):** Oku Ampofo's study trip to China helped shape Ghana's plant-medicine research centre (1975) [5].
