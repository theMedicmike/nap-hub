# Siddha (Tamil South India)

*It is a medicine of the perfected ones. Its verses were scratched into palm leaves, its recipes were sometimes written in code, and its own physicians spent much of the last century arguing over how old it really is.*

---

## The legend

*What follows is the tradition's own account. It is sacred story, not a historical record.*

The story begins with a god. Tamil Siddha tradition says that Shiva taught the knowledge of healing to his consort Parvati. She gave it to Nandi, the great bull who carries Shiva, and Nandi passed it to the first human keepers, the **Siddhars**. In Tamil the word means "the perfected ones" or "the accomplished ones" [1][2].

Tradition counts eighteen of them. One list begins with Nandi and Agastya and runs through Tirumular, Bogar, Konkanavar, Kalangi, Theraiyar and the rest, ending with Kudhambai. Other lists give a slightly different order, and the tradition has never insisted on a single version [1].

The tallest figure in the story is **Agastya**, whom Tamil speakers call Agattiyar or Akattiyar. He is revered across India. In the south he is more than a sage. Tamil tradition credits him with the origin of medicine *and* of the Tamil language itself [1]. Scholars call him "semi-legendary" [1]. Tamil physicians of the 1920s and 1930s told and retold how the eighteen Siddhars, led by Agastya, gathered on Pothigai hill, a peak in the far south of the Western Ghats [3].

One small legend shows how the tradition remembers its own skill. A sage, the story goes, had a toad living inside his skull. Agastya opened the skull, but it was his disciple who, with an instrument, made the toad jump out into a bowl of water. Agastya named the clever student Theraiyar, from *thérai*, "toad" [1]. Historians notice that this story has a cousin far to the north: Buddhist texts tell of the physician Jivaka opening a merchant's skull, and that tale travelled across Buddhist Asia [1].

Another Siddhar, **Bogar**, is linked to the hill temple at Palani. Devotional tradition says he made its image of the god Murugan from nine minerals, and that he journeyed to China [7]. Historians have not confirmed the China journey or any of the dates attached to Bogar, and the dates people give him lie centuries apart. It belongs here, in the legend.

## What the record shows

The written record is younger and more tangled than the legend, and an honest story has to say so.

**Early echoes.** Medicine reached Tamil literature early, but it came under a Sanskrit name. The *Cilappatikaram*, a Tamil epic that Zysk places in the mid-fifth century CE (other scholars date it somewhat later), already refers to Ayurveda, using the Tamil form *āyulvētar* [1]. The *Tirukkural*, a famous book of Tamil couplets dated to around 450–550 CE, mentions the three humours, the same three bodily forces that Ayurveda describes [1].

**The first Siddha text.** Scholars name the *Tirumandiram* of Tirumular as the first Tamil Siddha text. It probably dates to the sixth or seventh century CE. It already mentions alchemy, for example turning iron into gold, but it contains no specific medical doctrine [1].

**The medical works.** The writings that make up Siddha medicine came out of ascetic groups called *Kayasiddhas*, "those perfected in the body." They pursued a body that would not decay through yoga, alchemy, medicine and Tantric ritual. Zysk dates their works, attributed to Agastya and many others, from about the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries onward. The historian Kenneth Zysk reports that most critical scholars agree, judging by the language, that the texts presenting Siddha as a full *system* of healing cannot be older than the sixteenth century [1]. Another scholar, reviewing the texts in 2021, places their composition between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries and stresses that no reliable data fix their dates [6].

**The great dating argument.** Some modern Siddha writers give far older dates. Tamil physicians of the 1920s traced their medicine back twelve thousand years, tying it to the lost continent of Lemuria [3]. Zysk warns that most secondary sources on Siddha's history were written by Siddha doctors themselves, at a time of strong Tamil cultural pride, and that the claims about age should be read in that light [1]. The fair summary is this. The healing practices are clearly old, and some reach back before the texts. The written system, as it now exists, took shape around the sixteenth century.

**The twentieth-century twist.** The most surprising finding is recent. Until the early 1900s, many Tamil practitioners simply called their medicine *Āyuḷvētam*, Ayurveda. A 2023 study in the journal *Medical History* shows how "Siddha" emerged as a distinct, named *system* in the Madras Presidency during the 1920s and 1930s [3]. Three forces drove it: Tamil revivalism, the anti-Brahmin Dravidian movement, and colonial government inquiries. The Madras Government formed a medical committee under Khan Bahadur Muhammad Usman on 10 August 1921, and its inquiry gave Tamil physicians a public stage to argue for their own tradition. In 1924 an Indigenous Medical School opened in Madras [3]. The authors conclude that Siddha's identity as a separate system is largely a twentieth-century phenomenon. The knowledge inside it is much older [3].

## Keepers of the knowledge

**Agastya (Agattiyar).** In tradition he is the founder and the first guru, a sage of the southern mountains. In the written record his name sits on many medical works from the thirteenth century onward [1]. These are works *attributed* to him, which is a common way of honouring a teacher in Indian literature. Whether one historical Agastya wrote any of them, historians cannot say.

**Tirumular.** He is the poet-yogi of the *Tirumandiram* [1]. His verses join devotion, yoga and the dream of a perfected body. For historians he is the earliest firm anchor in the Siddha story.

**Theraiyar.** There are two of them, and the record keeps them apart. One is the legendary disciple who coaxed the toad out of the skull. The other is a real medical author of about the late seventeenth century, said to have written twelve works on medicine [1].

**Iramatevar, who became Yakkopu.** Theraiyar's best-known disciple is one of the most remarkable figures in the story. In the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century, Zysk records, he travelled to Mecca, studied there, embraced Islam and took the name Yakkopu, which is Jacob [1]. A Tamil alchemist-physician on pilgrimage to Arabia shows how far these healers moved and how open their world could be.

**Bogar.** He is the alchemist of Palani and the traveller of legend. Some modern writers place him as late as the sixteenth or seventeenth century; popular sources place him more than a thousand years earlier [7]. No firm evidence settles it, and historians are unsure.

**The twentieth-century builders.** Modern Siddha also has founders whose names are documented. They include **Virudai Sivagnana Yogi**, who founded the Tiruvitar Kazhakam in 1908; **S. S. Anandam**, who formed the South India Ayurveda Association in 1914; and the physicians of the Dravida Vaidya Mandal, founded in 1918. Together they argued before colonial committees that Tamil medicine deserved its own name and its own schools [3].

## How it was passed down

For most of its life, Siddha knowledge travelled in three ways: as **verse**, through **families**, and on **leaves**.

**Verse and code.** The Siddhars wrote in Tamil poetic metre, and verse is easy to memorise and recite. Much of it was also meant to be hard to read. Many recipes use metaphor and coded language, so that only a student taught by a teacher could unlock them. A recent study notes that the knowledge was meant to pass down only within one's own line, so the meaning was given "in an implicit and coded way" [8]. Zysk describes how the key ingredient *muppu* is still kept secret, known only to the guru and taught only when a student is judged ready [1].

**Families and apprenticeship.** Siddha was long a hereditary art. A father taught a son at the bedside, and the student learned by watching, mixing and feeling pulses for years. Zysk describes how a student, "after long periods of practice under the guidance of a skilled teacher," slowly learns to feel subtle differences under each of three fingertips [1]. Among Natar families of Kanyakumari district, the bone-setting and pressure-point art called *varmam* was passed "from generation to generation by word of mouth" [1].

**Palm leaves.** Picture the scene. A scribe holds a dried, trimmed strip of palm leaf and cuts letters into it with a stylus [8]. The scratched letters are hard to see until the leaf is rubbed with a dark mixture of charcoal and lemongrass oil, and then the black settles into the grooves and the verse appears [9]. Palm leaves are fragile. Insect larvae, rodents and fungus attack them, so the texts survived only as long as people kept copying them [9].

**What was lost.** The people working to record these manuscripts give a hard detail. Many bundles lie neglected in family homes, because few practitioners today can still read the old script and coded verse [9]. When a family line of healers ends, its leaves can end with it.

**Rescue.** In 1997 UNESCO inscribed the **Tamil Medical Manuscript Collection** of the Institute of Asian Studies in Chennai on its Memory of the World Register [4]. That collection is mostly Tamil medical palm-leaf texts. The Institute also became a regional training centre for conserving and digitising palm-leaf manuscripts [4]. A British Library Endangered Archives project, run with the French Institute of Pondicherry, has digitised more than 1,500 Siddha manuscripts [9].

## How it traveled

Siddha's movements are a story of meetings as much as roads.

**Sharing a house with Ayurveda.** Siddha and Ayurveda share their theory. That includes the five elements, the three humours (in Tamil, *vatham*, *pittam* and *siletuman*), the seven body tissues and the five vital winds. Zysk writes that Siddha "relied entirely on Ayurveda" for the doctrines linking body and nature [1]. Siddha adjusted them for Tamil country. It reverses Ayurveda's order of the humours across a lifetime and allows for the hot, wet climate of the south [1].

**Alchemy, and a debated gift.** Alchemy appears in Tirumular's text by the sixth or seventh century. In North India it became *Rasashastra*, "the knowledge of mercury." Because alchemy grew far more elaborate in Siddha than in classical Ayurveda, Zysk reports a widely held belief that medical alchemy "may well have begun in South India" and was later absorbed into Ayurveda [1]. Tamil physicians of the 1920s went further. Virudai Sivagnana Yogi argued that the Ayurveda of Charaka and Sushruta used no minerals or metals, and that later alchemists learned them from Tamil books [3]. Both views remain open, and neither has been proven.

**The Islamic world.** Zysk argues that pulse reading and urine examination, both central in Siddha, "in all probability" came from Unani, the medicine of the Islamic world, rooted in Greek and Arabic learning [1]. Iramatevar's journey to Mecca shows the human side of that exchange [1].

**Across the Palk Strait.** Tamil communities in Sri Lanka carried Siddha with them. A Diploma in Siddha Medicine was offered alongside Ayurveda and Unani at the College of Indigenous Medicine in Borella, Colombo, from 1929. On 2 July 1984 the Siddha section was transferred to the University of Jaffna, and in July 2024 it was gazetted as a full Faculty of Siddha Medicine [10].

## Signature ideas and plants

**The worldview in one sentence.** The body is made of the same five elements as the universe, three forces keep it in balance, and the highest goal is a body so well tuned that it does not decay [1].

**Eight ways of looking.** Siddha diagnosis traditionally examines eight things: tongue, complexion, voice, eyes, touch, stool, pulse and urine [1]. One examination is distinctive. The physician lets a drop of sesame (gingelly) oil fall onto a urine sample and reads its spreading shape, which may be a ring, a line, a lotus or a spear [1]. Pulse reading is described with animals. A healthy "wind" pulse moves like a swan or a peacock, a "bile" pulse like a tortoise or a leech, and a "phlegm" pulse like a frog or a snake [1].

**Kayakalpa: the body made durable.** The most distinctive branch is *kayakalpa*, rejuvenation. It combines breath control and yoga, discipline of the body, the secret salt *muppu*, calcined metal and mineral powders, and plant drugs special to each physician [1].

**Metals, minerals and a warning.** Siddha's pharmacy is famous, and controversial, for its minerals. Mercury and sulphur are its "cornerstones," and tradition likens them to Shiva and Parvati. They are joined by gold, copper, iron, arsenic compounds, mica and more. Metals are "purified" and then "killed" by repeated heating in fires of dried cow dung, sometimes many times over [1]. Zysk states plainly that strong scientific evidence shows most of these metals and minerals to be toxic to the human body. Practitioners say their traditional processing makes them safe [1]. This chapter tells that history. It does not endorse it.

**Plants as history.** Siddha physicians kept a materia medica of at least 108 plants and plant products, some brought from as far as the Himalayas [1]. Plants also served as chemical tools. Particular plant juices and oils, such as the sap of *Jatropha curcas*, the little sorrel *Oxalis corniculata*, palmyra toddy and mahua (*Bassia longifolia*) oil, were each assigned to "purify" a different metal before it was burned [1]. Sesame oil was used for reading urine, and lemongrass oil went into the dark mixture that made the manuscripts readable [1][9]. Datura appears among the herbs in Siddha shops [1]. None of these is presented here as a cure. They are part of the history of what people grew, ground and believed.

**Other specialties.** Siddha developed expertise in eyes, with two treatises that catalogue ninety-six eye conditions, in poisons and snakebite, and in *varmam*, the vital points of the body. Varmam grew up alongside the southern martial art of staff fighting, *silambam* [1]. Surgery with the knife, so prominent in Sushruta's Ayurveda, hardly appears [1].

## Today

Siddha is a living, government-recognised tradition, centred in Tamil Nadu and the Tamil communities of Sri Lanka.

- In India it is one of the systems overseen by the **Ministry of Ayush**. The **National Institute of Siddha** in Tambaram, Chennai, was inaugurated on 3 September 2005 and is affiliated with the Tamil Nadu Dr. M.G.R. Medical University [11].
- India has observed a national **Siddha Day** since 4 January 2018. It honours Agastya's birth star, Ayilyam, in the Tamil month of Margazhi [11].
- In Sri Lanka, the University of Jaffna's Faculty of Siddha Medicine trains Siddha physicians for the Bachelor of Siddha Medicine and Surgery degree [10].
- The palm-leaf heritage is being conserved and digitised in Chennai and by international projects [4][9].

There is honest debate too. Scholars note how much of Siddha's popular history comes from its own practitioners [1][3]. Its mineral medicines raise real safety questions [1]. The anthropologist Richard Weiss describes how modern Siddha draws strength from the idea of a pure, ancient Tamil golden age. He treats that vision as a real cultural force that shapes how the tradition collects and rebuilds itself [5]. Siddha today is old knowledge carried in a new frame, and it is still being reassembled.

## Threads to other traditions

- **From Ayurveda to Siddha** (by c. 450–550 CE onward): the three humours, five elements, seven tissues and vital winds. Siddha "relied entirely on Ayurveda" for core doctrine [1].
- **From Siddha to Ayurveda?** (debated): medical alchemy may have developed first among South Indian Siddha ascetics and later entered Ayurveda's Rasashastra. This is a widely held belief, not proven [1].
- **From Unani (Greek-Arabic medicine) to Siddha** (probably after c. the 14th century, when these methods also appear in Ayurveda): pulse reading and urine examination, "in all probability" [1].
- **Shared with Buddhist medicine** (Pali canon onward): the skull-opening healer story, told of Jivaka in Buddhist texts and carried in Sanskrit, Tibetan and Chinese versions [1].
- **From Babylonian and Greco-Roman astrology** (date uncertain): the head-to-toe ordering of the zodiac on the body [1].
- **Siddha meets Islam** (late 17th or early 18th century): Iramatevar studies in Mecca and becomes Yakkopu [1].
- **Siddha to Sri Lanka** (taught formally from 1929 in Colombo, and from 1984 in Jaffna) [10].
- **Siddha to China** (legend only): Bogar's voyage, which historians have not confirmed [7].
