# The Islamic Golden Age and Unani

*For about three hundred years, the medicine of Greece, Persia and India came together in Arabic, grew, and then went back out to the world. One of the places it lives on today has a name that simply means "Greek."*

## The legend

Most healing traditions start with a god or a sage-king. This one starts with a library.

The story people tell goes like this. In Baghdad, the round capital of the Abbasid caliphs, there was a "House of Wisdom" (*Bayt al-Hikma*). Scholars of every faith sat side by side in it. Christians, Muslims, Jews and Zoroastrians turned the old books of the world into Arabic, and a caliph paid for each translation by weighing it in gold. Scribes worked through the night by lamplight. Brittle Greek scrolls came back from Byzantium on camels. The story is bright and full, and it is told with real affection.

Like most founding stories, it holds truth wrapped in shine. Historians agree that a great translation movement happened in Baghdad. They are much less sure what the House of Wisdom actually was. It may have been a palace library, an academy, or something in between, and later writers probably made it grander than it was. Even the most famous translator's link to it is debated. None of Hunayn ibn Ishaq's surviving translations credit the House of Wisdom, and at least one scholar has argued that there is no evidence he ever ran it [1][2]. So read the gold-for-books scene as legend, a way of saying *these people treasured knowledge*, and not as an account of what happened.

## What the record shows

**Gondishapur, before Baghdad.** In Khuzistan, in southwestern Iran, stood the city of Jundi-Shapur, usually written Gondishapur today. The Persian king Shapur I (reigned 241–272 CE) founded it and settled Greek captives there. Later sources say that around 555 CE the king Khusraw Anushirawan set up a medical school and hospital there [3]. Some modern historians think those later accounts made the school sound bigger and more formal than it was. What is solid is this: Gondishapur was a meeting place where Greek medicine, read in Syriac, sat next to Persian and Indian learning. Physicians trained there carried that mix into the Islamic world [3].

**The translation movement (late 700s–900s).** Under the Abbasid caliphs, Greek medical and philosophical works were translated into Arabic, often by way of Syriac, on a huge scale. The central figure was **Hunayn ibn Ishaq** (808–873; some sources give 877 for his death). He was a Christian scholar from al-Hira in Iraq, known in Latin as *Johannitius*. His translations made Galen, Hippocrates, Plato and Aristotle readable in Arabic [1]. Sources disagree on how many works of Galen he translated. Counts range from about ninety-five to more than a hundred, and much of the work was shared with his son and his nephew [2]. Historians do agree that he worked like a careful scholar of texts. He compared several manuscripts before he trusted one.

**India came too.** The Barmakids were a powerful family of viziers under Caliph Harun al-Rashid. They invited Indian scholars to Baghdad. Medieval biographers, among them Ibn Abi Usaybi'a, name an Indian physician, Manka, who is said to have helped put the *Sushruta Samhita*, the great Sanskrit surgical text, into Persian and then Arabic [4]. The details are disputed: who translated what, in which order, and whether the *Charaka Samhita* was fully translated as well. In the early 1000s, though, the scholar al-Biruni was able to use old Arabic versions of both Sushruta and Charaka [5]. This Indian current seems to have weakened after the Barmakids fell from power in 803, when Greek learning became the court's main focus [4].

**Hospitals with records.** The *bimaristan* (a Persian word meaning roughly "place of the sick") became the institution at the heart of this medicine. Caliph Harun al-Rashid (reigned 786–809) is said to have invited Jibrail ibn Bukhtishu, from a famous Gondishapur family of physicians, to build a hospital in Baghdad, often called the city's first [3]. Later bimaristans had separate wards for men and women, and for fevers, wounds, eye disease and mental illness. They also had pharmacies, libraries and lecture halls. Medicine and food were free, and some accounts say hospitals hired musicians to comfort patients [3]. In 931, the story goes, a patient died through a physician's mistake, and Caliph al-Muqtadir ordered his physician Sinan ibn Thabit to examine the city's practitioners. A medieval chronicle by Sinan's own son puts the number examined in Baghdad at more than 860 [17]. Some historians suspect the account flatters Sinan and may not match reality exactly [17]. Either way, the idea that a doctor should be tested before treating the public was taking root. In 1284 the Mamluk sultan al-Mansur Qalawun founded the al-Mansuri hospital in Cairo, and it became a model for later bimaristans [3].

## Keepers of the knowledge

**Abu Bakr al-Razi (865–925): the man who watched.** He was born in Rayy, near modern Tehran, and died there, and he became a leading physician in Baghdad [6]. In Latin he was called *Rhazes*. His huge notebook-encyclopedia, *al-Hawi* ("The Comprehensive"), mixes quotations from older authors with his own patient cases [6]. His short book on smallpox and measles is often called the first clear clinical description of smallpox. In it he separated the two illnesses by what he saw at the bedside. Smallpox, he noted, came with marked back pain and fever. Measles brought more restlessness and nausea [6][7]. He also suggested a test of bloodletting that sounds startlingly modern: bleed one group of patients, leave a similar group alone, and compare what happens [6]. A well-loved story says he chose the site for a Baghdad hospital by hanging pieces of meat around the city and picking the spot where they rotted most slowly [3][6]. Historians are unsure the story is true. The versions don't agree on which hospital or which ruler, and the most popular version ties it to the 'Adudi hospital, which opened around 980, roughly fifty years after al-Razi died [18]. If there is truth in it, it more likely belongs to an earlier Baghdad hospital. The story lasts because it fits the man.

**Ibn Sina (980–1037): the great organizer.** In Latin he was *Avicenna*. He was born in a village near Bukhara, in today's Uzbekistan, and died in Hamadan, in Iran [8]. Around 1025 he completed *al-Qanun fi al-Tibb*, the *Canon of Medicine*, in five parts. The first covers general principles. The second covers medicinal substances. The third and fourth cover diseases of particular organs and of the whole body. The fifth covers compound remedies [8]. Ibn Sina did not set out to be original. His aim was a system, Galen and Aristotle fitted together into one orderly whole, and that orderliness is what made the *Canon* so easy to teach. Its Latin translation by Gerard of Cremona became the standard version [8]. Printed Latin editions followed in the 1470s, and an Arabic edition was printed in Rome in 1593 [8]. The *Canon* stayed a standard textbook in European universities well into the 1600s, and a few kept it on the syllabus even later [9][19].

**Abu al-Qasim al-Zahrawi (c. 936–c. 1013): the surgeon of Córdoba.** In Latin he was *Albucasis*. He lived and worked in Córdoba, in Muslim Spain (al-Andalus). He wrote a medical encyclopedia, the *Kitab al-Tasrif*, in thirty sections [10]. The last section, on surgery, made his name. It describes and draws about two hundred instruments: scalpels, hooks, forceps, saws, needles, and wooden devices for setting broken bones [10]. Those pictures are among the earliest known drawings of surgical tools. He built on the Byzantine surgeon Paul of Aegina and on his own long practice. In Latin translation, his surgery guided European surgeons for centuries [10].

**Ibn al-Nafis (1213–1288): the man who said no to Galen.** He trained at the Nuri hospital in Damascus and later became chief physician at the al-Mansuri hospital in Cairo [11]. When he was about twenty-nine he wrote a *Commentary on Anatomy in Avicenna's Canon*. In it he denied Galen's idea that blood seeps through tiny pores in the wall between the heart's two lower chambers. In his words, "there is no passage between these two cavities" [11]. The blood, he reasoned, had to travel from the right side of the heart *through the lungs* to the left. This is pulmonary circulation, the "small circuit." He even supposed there were small connections inside the lung between artery and vein, roughly four centuries before Malpighi saw capillaries under a microscope [11]. Then the manuscript sat quietly in libraries. It was rediscovered in 1924 in Berlin by a doctoral student, Muhyo al-Deen al-Tatawi [11]. Michael Servetus published a similar idea in Europe in 1553, and William Harvey later described the full circulation. Whether any European knew of Ibn al-Nafis's work is still debated. The likeness is close enough to raise the question, but many historians believe Servetus reached his idea on his own, in which case both men deserve the credit [11][12].

## How it was passed down

This was a tradition of the book, but never the book alone.

- **Translation chains.** A Greek text often went into Syriac first and then into Arabic. A Sanskrit text often went into Persian first [2][4]. Each step was a set of hands, and each step could add notes, mistakes or new ideas.
- **Commentary.** The usual way to make progress was to write a commentary on a respected book. Ibn al-Nafis's great challenge to Galen is, in form, a commentary on Ibn Sina [11]. Teachers explained, disagreed, and wrote it all down in the margins.
- **Hospitals as schools.** Bimaristans had lecture halls and libraries. Students followed physicians through the wards and kept records of patients and treatments [3]. The leading physicians of the age, al-Razi and Ibn Sina among them, are remembered as hospital directors and teachers [3].
- **Families and masters.** Medicine often ran in families. The Bukhtishu family served the caliphs across several generations [3]. Hunayn worked with his son and nephew [2]. Learning passed from master to apprentice as well as from page to reader.
- **Memorized verse.** Ibn Sina also wrote a medical poem, the *Urjuza*, a teaching text set in rhyme so students could memorize it [13]. It was later translated into Latin too.
- **Exams and licenses.** According to the chronicle of 931, practicing in Baghdad now meant passing an examination [3][17]. The knowledge was gaining gatekeepers.

## How it traveled

The movement went in two directions: inward to Baghdad, then outward to Europe.

**Inward.** Greek medicine arrived through Syriac-speaking Christian scholars and the Gondishapur world [1][3]. Persian practice came with Gondishapur's physicians [3]. Indian texts arrived under Barmakid patronage, often through Persian [4][5]. In Arabic, these streams mixed.

**Westward, across the Mediterranean.** Two gateways mattered most.

- **Southern Italy.** Constantine the African (c. 1020–1087; historians are unsure of his death date) came from North Africa and ended his life as a monk at Monte Cassino. There he translated many Arabic medical works into Latin [14]. The best known is the *Pantegni*, a shortened version of the *Kitab al-Maliki* by the Persian physician ʿAli ibn al-ʿAbbas al-Majusi [14]. His Latin versions circulated in the medical world of nearby Salerno. Historians have long criticized how loosely he translated, and how seldom he named his sources [14].
- **Spain.** After Toledo came under Christian rule, it became a center for translating Arabic into Latin. Gerard of Cremona, an Italian who settled there in the 1100s, put dozens of Arabic scientific works into Latin, among them Ibn Sina's *Canon* [15]. (A few works once credited to him may belong to a later namesake, Gerard of Sabbioneta [15].)

From these doors the Arabic tradition, with Galen inside it, became the backbone of medical teaching at Europe's young universities [8][9]. Al-Razi's smallpox book alone appeared in Latin and other European languages for several centuries [6][7].

**Eastward, to South Asia.** Over the following centuries, physicians trained in this Greco-Arabic medicine (*hakims*) moved into the Indian subcontinent and found patrons at royal courts. There they met Ayurveda again. This was the same Indian learning that had once traveled to Baghdad. In India the tradition became known as *Unani* or *Unani Tibb*.

## Signature ideas and plants

**The name.** *Unani* comes from the Arabic word for "Greek," which goes back to "Ionian." The tradition names itself after its Greek source, which is an unusually honest way to describe where it came from.

**Four humors, one balance.** At its core is the Greek idea, refined by Galen and organized by Ibn Sina, that the body holds four humors: blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile [8]. Each person has a *mizaj*, a temperament, made from their own mix of hot, cold, moist and dry. Health is that mix in balance. Illness is the mix out of balance.

**The six essentials.** Physicians looked closely at six areas of daily life: the air you breathe, food and drink, movement and rest, sleep and waking, what the body keeps in and lets out, and emotions. Much of the practice was about adjusting these, with drugs coming after.

**The pharmacy.** The second book of the *Canon* is a materia medica, a catalog of medicinal substances [8]. Arabic pharmacy drew on Dioscorides' Greek herbal, translated into Arabic, and on Indian and Persian plants and spices. Al-Biruni later wrote a whole book on pharmacognosy (the study of medicinal substances) that brought these streams together [5]. The traces remain in everyday English. *Syrup* comes from Arabic *sharab*, a drink. *Julep* goes back through Arabic to Persian *gulab*, rosewater. These are words about how medicine was made and taken. They are history, not instructions.

None of this is offered here as proof that any substance treats any illness. It is the record of how people reasoned about health a thousand years ago.

## Today

Unani is practiced today mainly in South Asia, and most of all in India. Its modern revival owes much to **Hakim Ajmal Khan** of Delhi. In the early 1900s (sources give 1906 or 1910) he founded a Tibbi Conference to bring physicians together, and he built institutions, among them the Ayurvedic and Unani Tibbia College in Delhi [16]. That college taught both traditions under one roof. Its foundation stone was laid in 1916, and Mahatma Gandhi formally opened it in 1921 [16]. The Ajmal Khan Tibbiya College at Aligarh Muslim University dates from 1927 [16].

In 1995 the Government of India set up a Department of Indian Systems of Medicine and Homoeopathy. In 2003 it was renamed AYUSH (Ayurveda, Yoga and Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha and Homoeopathy), and in 2014 it became a full ministry [20]. A Central Council for Research in Unani Medicine works under it, and since 2017 India has marked Unani Day each year on 11 February, Hakim Ajmal Khan's birthday [21]. Traditional use, and a tradition's long life, are not the same as tested evidence, and this chapter tells that story as heritage.

## Threads to other traditions

- **Received from Greek and Roman medicine (from the late 700s):** Hippocrates and Galen, translated into Arabic, often by way of Syriac, by Hunayn ibn Ishaq and his circle in Abbasid Baghdad [1][2].
- **Received from Persian medicine (500s–700s):** the hospital model and physician families of Gondishapur, carried to Baghdad by the Bukhtishu family [3].
- **Received from Ayurveda (late 700s–early 800s):** the *Sushruta Samhita*, translated into Persian and Arabic under Barmakid patronage; Arabic versions of Sushruta and Charaka were still read by al-Biruni in the 1000s [4][5].
- **Gave to European monastic and Salernitan medicine (late 1000s):** Constantine the African's Latin translations at Monte Cassino, including the *Pantegni* [14].
- **Gave to European universities (1100s into the 1600s):** Gerard of Cremona's Latin *Canon* from Toledo, a standard textbook in Europe well into the 1600s [8][9][15][19].
- **Gave to European surgery (medieval period onward):** al-Zahrawi's surgical book and its instrument drawings, in Latin translation [10].
- **Ran parallel to European anatomy (1200s / 1500s):** Ibn al-Nafis's pulmonary circulation came before Servetus (1553) and Harvey. Whether Europeans knew of it is debated [11][12].
- **Rejoined Ayurveda in South Asia (medieval to modern):** became Unani Tibb, taught today beside Ayurveda in Indian colleges and under the same national ministry [16].
