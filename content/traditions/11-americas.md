# Indigenous medicine of the Americas

*Two continents, hundreds of nations, and knowledge from forest and mountain that found its way into modern operating rooms, some of it carried there on the tip of a hunting dart.*

## The legend

There is no single founding legend here, and that is the first thing to understand. The Americas were never one tradition. From the Arctic to Tierra del Fuego lived hundreds of nations, each with its own language, its own healers, and its own account of where healing came from. Many of those accounts belong to their communities and are not told to outsiders. This chapter respects that. It does not retell them.

What we can share are the beginnings that communities and the written record have made public.

The Kallawaya, travelling healers of the Bolivian Andes, trace their roots to a time before the Inca. That is how they tell their own story, and UNESCO repeats it in its official description of their heritage [2].

In the Yucatán, Maya healing words called on the Bacabs. In Maya belief these were the beings who hold up the sky, one at each corner of the world. Their name is on a book of healing words that survived the colonial centuries, copied by hand in the Maya language [9].

And then there is a legend that Europeans told about the Americas. Around 1630, the story goes (some versions say 1638), the Countess of Chinchón, wife of the Spanish viceroy of Peru, lay burning with fever in Lima. She was given a powder made from the bark of a mountain tree and recovered, and the grateful world named the tree after her. It is a lovely story. Historians have, in Cambridge University Library's words, "almost completely rejected" it [3]. The tree's real story is older, and it belongs to the people of the Andes.

## What the record shows

The oldest evidence is not writing. It is leaves.

Under house floors in the Nanchoc Valley of northern Peru, archaeologist Tom Dillehay and his team found coca leaves dated to at least 8,000 years ago. With them were calcium-rich stones that had been burned and scraped to make lime, the powder still chewed with coca today to draw out the leaf's active compounds. The leaves turned up in certain households only, which the team took as a sign of a practice with social meaning, not a casual habit [8]. Before this find, the earliest evidence for coca chewing was roughly 3,000 years younger [8].

Writing came to medicine in the Americas mostly after 1519, and mostly under Spanish rule. That makes the record both precious and tangled: Indigenous knowledge, written down by Indigenous scholars, often in Latin or Spanish, for European readers.

The jewel is a small book finished in 1552 at the Colegio de Santa Cruz in Tlatelolco, a school in Mexico City founded to educate sons of the Nahua (Aztec) nobility. Its author was **Martín de la Cruz**, a Nahua physician at the college. His colleague **Juan Badiano**, also Nahua, translated it into Latin. Its title is *Libellus de Medicinalibus Indorum Herbis*, "A Little Book of the Medicinal Herbs of the Indians," and today people often call it the Badianus Manuscript or the Codex de la Cruz-Badiano [1]. It is often described as the earliest known herbal written by an Indigenous author in the Americas. Painted plants, their roots spread out in the Nahua style, sit beside Nahuatl names and Latin text. Martín de la Cruz's original Nahuatl version is lost. Only Badiano's Latin survives [1].

Around the same years, the Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún and his Nahua collaborators were compiling the great encyclopedia now called the Florentine Codex. It includes healing plants alongside rituals and daily life. Sources date its completion anywhere from 1569 to 1579 [10]. In 1570 King Philip II of Spain sent his physician Francisco Hernández to New Spain to catalogue its plants and animals, a survey that took him about seven years of travel [10].

For the Maya, no surviving hieroglyphic text deals plainly with medicine. Most of what we know comes from colonial manuscripts [9]. The best known, the *Ritual of the Bacabs*, holds some forty-two main incantations in Yucatec Maya, with fragments in between. Princeton's catalogue dates the handwriting to between about 1775 and 1800 and notes that it was copied from a manuscript of the 1500s or 1600s, so its roots run deeper than its paper. Even so, scholars are unsure exactly how old the ideas in it are [9].

## Keepers of the knowledge

**Martín de la Cruz** (active 1552). We know almost nothing about the man himself except his work, and his work says a great deal. Picture a Nahua healer, trained in his own people's medicine, sitting in a colonial school a generation after the conquest, setting down what he knew so that it would not vanish. His own Nahuatl words are gone. The book survives because a colleague put it into Latin [1].

**The healers of Tlatelolco.** The herbal section of Book XI of the Florentine Codex ends with something rare in colonial records: the names of the experienced healers of Tlatelolco who supplied its plant knowledge. According to the text, they could not write, and they asked a scribe to set their names down [14]. Five centuries later, they are still credited.

**The Kallawaya.** They are travelling healers from the mountainous Bautista Saavedra region north of La Paz, Bolivia. UNESCO describes the healers as exclusively men. Kallawaya women take part in a number of rites and care for pregnant women and children. UNESCO says their pharmacopoeia, "with some 980 species" of plants, "rates as one of the richest in the world" [2].

**Manuel Mamani.** He was a Bolivian who knew the cinchona forests. In the 1860s he helped the English trader Charles Ledger obtain seeds of an unusually rich strain of the "fever tree." The seeds went on to found plantations on the other side of the world. Mamani paid for it: according to Cambridge University Library he was "jailed, beaten, and eventually starved to death" for his part [3]. His name belongs in this chapter as much as any professor's.

**Richard Evans Schultes** (1915–2001). He was born in East Boston, entered Harvard on a scholarship, and earned his doctorate in 1941. That year he went to southeastern Colombia on a grant to study arrow poisons [5]. Then the war came, and with Southeast Asia's rubber cut off, the American embassy directed him to stay and search the Amazon for disease-resistant, high-yielding rubber trees. He stayed more than a decade. Harvard Magazine credits him with the first detailed studies of the ayahuasca vine, and a photograph caption names the healer Salvador Chindoy as the man who introduced him to it. Over his career he collected more than 24,000 plant specimens. Back at Harvard he lectured in a hall hung with masks, dance costumes and blowguns. He warned that Indigenous peoples and their plant knowledge were disappearing "faster than the forest itself" [5]. He is remembered as a founding father of rainforest conservation and one of the great figures of ethnobotany, the study of how peoples use plants [5].

## How it was passed down

For most of history, and in most nations still, the knowledge lived in people rather than on paper. It passed from teacher to apprentice and from elder to younger relative, through spoken teaching, song, and years of walking the land beside someone who knew it.

UNESCO describes Kallawaya learning as "a complex system of transmission and apprenticeship in which the journey plays an essential role" [2]. A young healer learns by travelling, meeting new plants and new places along the way. Music is part of the tradition too: groups called *kantus* play drum and pan flute during ritual ceremonies [2].

Then came the colonial manuscripts, a strange and fragile second chain. Nahua scholars wrote in Latin. Maya scribes copied healing texts over generations until one copy was found in the Yucatán in the winter of 1914–15. It is now at Princeton, and the first, and still the only, English translation, by Ralph Roys, was published in 1965 [9].

The Badianus manuscript's journey is a story in itself. It left Mexico and ended up in Rome, in the library of the Barberini family, which passed to the Vatican Library in 1902. There the little book sat largely unnoticed until the American scholar Charles Upson Clark drew attention to it in 1929 [15]. In 1990 Pope John Paul II returned it to Mexico (one account gives 1991). It is now in the care of Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History [1][15]. After roughly four centuries abroad, Martín de la Cruz's work came home.

## How it traveled

**Cinchona: from the Andes to the world.** Jesuit missionaries in Peru learned of the bark's power "from natives during the years 1620–1630," according to the Catholic Encyclopedia as quoted by Cambridge University Library [3]. Historians still debate exactly which peoples first used it, and how [3][7]. From 1650 Jesuit colleges in Genoa, Lyon, Louvain and Regensburg were using it. In Protestant England it met religious prejudice as "Popish powder," until Robert Talbor, who became physician to Charles II and later treated patients at the court of Louis XIV, made it fashionable [3]. In 1820 the French chemists Pierre Joseph Pelletier and Joseph Bienaimé Caventou isolated quinine from the bark [3][7]. Then empires came for the tree itself. A Dutch expedition collected seeds in 1852. A British one under Clements Markham followed in 1860, and by August 1861 cinchona was growing at Ootacamund in India. Ledger's seeds, gathered with Manuel Mamani's help and smuggled out in 1865, held about 8 to 13 percent quinine, against yields as low as 1.5 percent in earlier stock. They "destroyed the South American monopoly" on quinine [3]. In the early twentieth century, plantations in the Dutch East Indies supplied about 97 percent of the world's quinine [3]. Knowledge first shared in the Andes ended up making money for colonial plantations half a world away.

**Curare: from the hunting dart to the operating room.** Peoples of the Amazon and Guiana made curare, a dark plant paste for arrow and dart tips, to bring down game [4]. Europe heard about it early. Peter Martyr described poisoned arrows in 1516, and Walter Raleigh's account of Guiana in the 1590s mentions them; one of his officers wrote the name as "ourari" [4]. In 1814 Charles Waterton kept a curare-dosed donkey alive by inflating its lungs; it lived on with the name "Wouralia." In 1846 Claude Bernard published experiments showing that curare acts where nerve meets muscle [4]. In 1935 Harold King isolated its key compound, tubocurarine, from a museum sample [4]. Then Richard Gill, an American who had lived in Ecuador, brought out about twenty-five pounds of crude curare and offered it to the drug company Squibb [4]. On 23 January 1942, at the Homeopathic Hospital of Montreal, anaesthetist Harold Griffith and his resident Enid Johnson gave curare to a young man having his appendix removed. They reported it that July [4]. Muscle relaxants have been part of surgical anaesthesia ever since, and tubocurarine itself was later displaced by newer drugs [4]. Its name keeps a memory of the forest: "tubo" refers to "tube curare," the kind carried in bamboo tubes.

**Echinacea: from the prairie to the pharmacy.** Purple coneflower has been called the most widely used medicinal plant of the Plains peoples, and their uses for it are recorded in the published ethnobotanical literature [6]. The Oglala Lakota name has been recorded as *ichahpe-hu* [11]. From around the 1870s H. C. F. Meyer, a German-born medicine seller in Pawnee City, Nebraska, sold the root in "Meyer's Blood Purifier," a patent medicine whose label promised help for nearly everything. In the autumn of 1885 Meyer sent root to the pharmacist John Uri Lloyd, whose family identified the plant, and also wrote to John King, a physician of the Eclectic school, an American movement that leaned on plant remedies. Both men were sceptical at first. King went on to study it, and within a few years it had entered Eclectic practice [11][12][16]. How much Meyer learned directly from Plains people is not firmly documented [11].

**What travelled back.** The exchange went both ways, and unequally. European diseases devastated Indigenous nations, and colonial churchmen often treated Indigenous healing and divination as one suspect thing. The Spanish friar Alonso de Molina's 1571 Nahuatl dictionary glossed *ticitl*, the word for a healer, as "physician" and also as "diviner and caster of lots" [17]. Even so, healers took up Old World plants and Christian elements. The Kallawaya tradition itself grew, UNESCO notes, from "the fusion of native and Christian religions" [2].

## Signature ideas and plants

In plain English: across many nations, health was understood as balance. Balance within the body, and balance between a person, their community, the land, and the spirit world. A Kallawaya healer's knowledge of plants is bound up with ritual and belief [2]. Maya healing words place illness inside a whole cosmos held up by the Bacabs [9]. Healing was rarely only about a plant. It was about relationships.

Some plants that made history (told as history, never as cures):

- **Coca.** It has been chewed in the Andes for at least 8,000 years, and Dillehay's team describe it as a deeply rooted economic, social and religious tradition there [8].
- **Cinchona, the "fever tree."** Andean bark, Jesuit powder, French alkaloid, colonial plantation crop. Few plants have travelled so far or made so much money for others [3].
- **Curare.** Made from forest vines (one source plant is *Chondrodendron tomentosum*), it went from hunting poison to the anaesthetist's tray [4].
- **Purple coneflower (*Echinacea*).** Of central importance to Plains peoples, it reached American and later European pharmacy shelves [6][12].
- **The painted plants of the Badianus.** These are among the earliest surviving Indigenous-authored portraits of American medicinal plants, labelled in Nahuatl [1].

## Today

These traditions are not museum pieces. They are alive, and they belong to their peoples.

The Kallawaya's "Andean cosmovision" was proclaimed a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity by UNESCO in 2003 and inscribed on its Representative List in 2008 [2]. UNESCO names the threats plainly: acculturation, too little legal protection for Indigenous communities, and the policies of major pharmaceutical companies [2]. Some scholars add a gentler caution: official recognition can also freeze a living practice into one approved version.

That mention of pharmaceutical companies points to the great unfinished question of this chapter: **biopiracy**. The word describes taking traditional knowledge or living resources without consent and without sharing the rewards. Cinchona and Manuel Mamani are an old example of the pattern. The world's answer so far is the **Nagoya Protocol** on access and benefit-sharing. It was adopted in Nagoya, Japan, on 29 October 2010, and it took effect on 12 October 2014 [13]. Building on the 1992 Convention on Biological Diversity, it aims at "fair and equitable sharing" of the benefits from genetic resources. It also covers the traditional knowledge of Indigenous peoples and local communities connected to those resources, including their consent [13]. Hard arguments remain, especially over genetic information stored as digital data.

Echinacea's story has a modern chapter too: more than a century of wild prairie harvest for distant markets [12], and a growing conversation about conservation and about who should benefit.

Most of all, the knowledge lives on in nations and communities that keep it on their own terms. Some of it is shared publicly. Much of it is not, and that choice is theirs.

## Threads to other traditions

- **Americas → European and global medicine (1600s onward):** cinchona bark carried by Jesuits from Peru into Europe; quinine isolated in Paris in 1820 [3].
- **Americas → colonial Asia (1850s–1860s):** cinchona seeds taken by Dutch (1852) and British (1860) expeditions, and by Ledger with Mamani's help (1865), to plantations in India, Ceylon and Java [3].
- **Amazon → modern anaesthesia (1516–1942):** curare reports reach Europe; Bernard's experiments (1846), King's isolation (1935), Griffith and Johnson in Montreal (1942) [4].
- **Plains nations → American Eclectic medicine (1880s):** echinacea reaches Lloyd and King via H. C. F. Meyer in 1885 [11][12][16].
- **Nahua medicine → European botany (1552–1570s):** the Badianus manuscript in Latin, the Florentine Codex, and Philip II's survey by Francisco Hernández [1][10].
- **Spain and Christianity → Andean and Maya healing (from the 1500s):** Christian elements fused into Kallawaya practice [2]; Maya healing texts preserved in colonial manuscripts [9].
- **Indigenous Amazonian knowledge → 20th-century ethnobotany (1941 onward):** Richard Evans Schultes and his Indigenous teachers [5].
- **All of the above → modern law (2010–2014):** the Nagoya Protocol on benefit-sharing [13].
