# Pacific and Aboriginal Australian healing

*Some of the oldest medical libraries on Earth were never written down. They were carried in memory, in chant, and in the land itself.*

---

## The legend

*What follows is how these traditions describe their own beginnings, in the broad terms their communities share publicly. It is tradition, not dated history.*

Start with the simplest idea, the one a twelve-year-old can hold: in these traditions, healing doesn't begin with a book or a founder. It begins with a relationship between people, their ancestors, the spirit world and the land.

For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, knowledge of plants and healing is understood as part of Country. "Country" means more than landscape. It is the living web of land, waters, kin, law and story that a people belong to. The stories of how particular plants came to be, and who may use them, belong to particular peoples and places. Many of those stories are not meant for outsiders, so this chapter does not retell them. All we need to understand here is the idea underneath: the medicine was already in the land, and the people were taught to read it.

In Hawaiʻi, healing knowledge is understood as sacred, something received through prayer and passed from the gods through generations of kahuna. A kahuna is an expert or priest, and a *kahuna lāʻau lapaʻau* is an expert in plant medicine. Gathering a plant and giving it to someone were both done with *pule* (prayer) and *oli* (chant) [3]. The plant was never simply an ingredient. It was a partner in the healing.

Among Māori in Aotearoa New Zealand, illness was traditionally understood as a sign of disharmony, of something out of balance between a person and the natural and spiritual world. The *tohunga*, the expert healer, first worked out where that imbalance lay, then treated it both spiritually and physically [4]. Healing meant putting the world back in order.

Three peoples, three oceans of difference, and one thread running through all of them: the healer listens before acting, and the land is a teacher.

## What the record shows

Here the honest historian has to slow down. These are oral traditions, which means the oldest "texts" are living people. Archaeology, botany and colonial records give us edges and outlines. They don't give us the whole picture.

**Australia.** Aboriginal peoples have lived on the Australian continent for tens of thousands of years. Archaeologists still debate the exact figure, and different sites give different estimates. What isn't in doubt is that this is one of the longest continuous human connections to a single landscape anywhere. Knowledge of which plants soothe, which ones wound, and which ones kill was refined over that whole span, by trial, observation and teaching. None of it was written down by its own keepers before British colonisation began in 1788. What survives in writing comes later, mostly through colonists, scientists and, more recently and more importantly, through Aboriginal communities publishing their own knowledge on their own terms.

The colonial record is revealing, partly for what it ignored. A history published by the Australian Plants Society notes that Aboriginal people "knew many plants which were useful in curbing infections," yet early settlers on the whole were unwilling to try Aboriginal treatments and favoured plants that reminded them of the ones they knew from home [5]. Settlers did turn to some local plants. Scurvy was the first colonists' main health problem, and their most popular remedy for it was "bush tea" or "sweet tea" brewed from the leaves of native sarsaparilla (*Smilax glyciphylla*) [5]. And Denis Considen, a surgeon on the First Fleet, is credited with first noticing the usefulness of oil from the Sydney peppermint (*Eucalyptus piperita*); he sent a sample to the botanist Joseph Banks in England in 1788 [5].

**Hawaiʻi.** The Hawaiian Islands were settled by Polynesian voyagers centuries before Europeans arrived. Historians and archaeologists still debate the exact dates. One telling detail survives in the plants themselves: many of the plants most often used in lāʻau lapaʻau are not native to Hawaiʻi at all. They are "canoe plants", carried across thousands of miles of open Pacific in voyaging canoes [3]. The pharmacy came with the settlers.

The written record of the tradition begins with contact. Captain James Cook arrived in 1778. Christian missionaries followed in 1820. According to the historical accounts, many of them took healing for "black magic", confusing healing kahuna with *kahuna anaʻana*, practitioners of what outsiders called sorcery, and worked to abolish traditional medicine altogether [3]. Later, the Revised Laws of Hawaii imposed fines or prison for practising the healing arts without a licence [3]. Diseases arriving from abroad in the 1800s, including tuberculosis, measles and influenza, were new to the islands, and the old knowledge had no answers for them [3].

**Aotearoa New Zealand.** Rongoā Māori, the traditional Māori healing system, takes in plant remedies (*rongoā rākau*), massage (*mirimiri*) and prayer (*karakia*) [4]. Its written history begins with colonisation and, painfully, with law. In 1907 the New Zealand Parliament passed the Tohunga Suppression Act. It was introduced by James Carroll, a Māori MP and the Native Minister, and, according to published histories, had the support of the Māori members of Parliament, out of concern about self-styled healers who were causing harm [4][6]. There were few prosecutions and very few convictions. Its real effect was to drive tohunga underground [4][6]. The Act was repealed in 1962, after a review of laws that treated Māori and Europeans differently [4].

## Keepers of the knowledge

Most of the great healers of these traditions are known only to their own families and communities, and many would want it that way. This chapter names only people whose roles are on the public record.

**Papa Henry Auwae of Hawaiʻi.** Picture a man in his nineties standing among the plants, teaching from memory what most of the world had forgotten. Henry Auwae was a *kahuna lāʻau lapaʻau*, and Hawaiian sources honour him as a *poʻokela*, a master of Hawaiian medicine [7][10]. His obituary records that he learned his plant knowledge from his great-great-grandmother of the Pai family associated with Honokōhau, said to have been 107 years old [9]. In 1996, Kupuna Lāʻau Lapaʻau o Hawaiʻi incorporated as an organisation of traditional healers practising under him, with the goal of preserving Hawaiian herbal healing [7]. According to his students' project, he taught a five-year course on Oʻahu from 1996 to 2000 [8]. He died at the very end of 2000; he gave his age as 94, though accounts of it varied [9]. His student Roddy Akau, who carries the lineage on, remembers one teaching that says a great deal about the tradition: "no one can certify you, only Akua can" [8]. Akua means God or the divine. In other words, a healer's standing came from the source of the knowledge and the community's trust, not from a certificate.

**The tohunga of Aotearoa.** No single founder stands at the head of rongoā, and that is the point. Tohunga had no training college. They learned from elders or as apprentices to practising tohunga, and their communities recognised them as experts [4]. Even while the 1907 Act was in force, training carried on quietly and the knowledge kept growing [6].

**The Bundjalung people.** In northern New South Wales, the Bundjalung people are the community most often credited with the traditional use of the tree that the world now calls tea tree. The keepers here are a people, not one famous individual [1]. (The next sections tell what the published record says they did with it.)

## How it was passed down

Imagine a library with no shelves. Every volume lives in a person. When that person teaches, the book is copied. When that person dies untaught, the book is gone.

That is what oral transmission means, and it is far more exact than it sounds. In Hawaiʻi, kahuna began training young, and mastering lāʻau lapaʻau could take many decades [3]. Students learned to judge plants by taste, smell, colour, appearance and the place they grew. They learned to prepare them as poultices, salves, teas and applications to the skin. And they learned the chants and prayers that went with gathering and giving each one [3]. The chant was a memory tool and a sacred act at the same time.

In Aotearoa, knowledge passed by word of mouth from elder to apprentice [4]. Under the Tohunga Suppression Act, the identities of tohunga were kept secret and rongoā was not discussed outside Māori communities [6]. That silence protected the knowledge. It also made it fragile.

In Australia, knowledge of plants is held within kinship and Country, and different knowledge belongs to different people: women's and men's knowledge, knowledge for particular places, knowledge passed at particular stages of life. That is why the respectful path today runs through community-led publishing. When Aboriginal communities and organisations choose what to share, and how, the chain of transmission stays in the hands of its keepers.

Every one of these chains was nearly broken. Hawaiian licensing rules from 1919 required practitioners to give Western plant names, which many kahuna trained in ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi (the Hawaiian language) could not supply [3]. A language barrier became a legal one. Some kahuna kept practising in secret [10]. The fact that anything survived is a testament to families who refused to let the books close.

## How it traveled

The first great journey in this story happened on the open ocean. Polynesian navigators, reading stars, swells and birds, sailed double-hulled canoes across the Pacific, and they carried plants with them on purpose: ʻawa (kava), noni, kukui, ʻōlena (turmeric) and more [3]. Every canoe was a floating seed bank. When people reached a new island, the medicine garden came ashore with them.

Then came the encounters with Europe, which mostly ran one way. Colonists took samples. Considen's eucalyptus oil went to England in 1788 [5]. Hawaiian and Māori healing was pushed to the margins by missionaries and by law [3][4].

The most famous journey of all belongs to the tea tree. The Bundjalung people's use of the leaves is recorded in a major scientific review. Crushed leaves were inhaled for coughs and colds, or sprinkled on wounds before a poultice was applied, and soaked leaves made an infusion for sore throats or skin complaints [1]. In the 1920s, the chemist Arthur Penfold and his team at Sydney's Technological Museum, studying Australian essential oils with commercial potential, reported the high germicidal activity of the oil of *Melaleuca alternifolia* [5]. In laboratory comparisons published in the 1920s and 1930s, it was rated eleven times as active as phenol, the disinfectant yardstick of the day [1]. (That was a test-tube measure, not proof of what the oil does in a person.) An industry followed. At first, workers hand-cut leaves from wild stands and distilled them in wood-fired bush stills. Demand fell after the Second World War, revived in the 1970s, and was met with plantations in the 1970s and 1980s, mostly around Lismore in New South Wales, which is Bundjalung country [1].

One popular story says tea tree oil went into Australian soldiers' kits in the Second World War. The scientists who wrote the major review looked for evidence of this and found none [1]. What a 1946 statement from the Sydney Technological Museum does record is less romantic: large quantities of the oil went into machine cutting oils in the ammunition annexes [5]. That is a good example of how a legend grows around a true story.

## Signature ideas and plants

In plain English, the shared worldview is this: **health is balance, and healing is relationship.** Rongoā sees health as spiritual, psychological, physical and familial all at once [4]. Lāʻau lapaʻau links illness to a loss of *mana* (spiritual power) or *pono* (rightness, balance), and it sits alongside *lomilomi* (massage), *haha* (diagnosis by touch) and *hoʻoponopono*, a family practice of setting relationships right [3][10]. Plants matter, but they are never the whole story.

The plants below are history: what people used and recorded. They are not recommendations, and traditional use is not evidence that anything works.

- **ʻAwa / kava (*Piper methysticum*)**, a canoe plant central to Pacific life and ceremony [3].
- **Noni (*Morinda citrifolia*)**, **kukui (*Aleurites moluccanus*)** and **ʻōlena (turmeric)**, all carried or used in Hawaiian practice [3].
- **Māmaki** and **ʻuhaloa**, among the plants named in Hawaiian healing [3].
- **Kawakawa**, the Māori pepper tree. Its leaves were chewed or boiled in traditional practice [4].
- **Harakeke (flax)**, best known for weaving, also recorded as a poultice for wounds [4].
- **Koromiko**, **karamū** and **kūmarahou**, each with recorded traditional uses in rongoā [4].
- **Mānuka and kānuka**, which New Zealanders also call "tea trees" [4].
- **Tutu**, a reminder that the healers' knowledge was also knowledge of danger. Almost every part of the plant is poisonous, and it has caused deaths [4].
- **Tea tree (*Melaleuca alternifolia*)**, the Bundjalung leaf that became a global industry [1].

One small puzzle of names ties the region together. "Ti" or "ti tree" is a Māori and Samoan name for *Cordyline* plants, while Australians call their *Melaleuca* "tea trees" or "paperbarks" [1]. Across the Pacific, the same sounds point to different leaves. It is a reminder that each people named its own world.

## Today

The revival is real, and it is led by the communities themselves.

In Hawaiʻi, the cultural renaissance of the 1970s brought healers back into the open [3]. The 1919 Hawaiʻi Medicine Board was abolished in 1959, and the Native Hawaiian Health Care Act of 1988 recognised lāʻau lapaʻau as traditional medicine [3]. The University of Hawaiʻi's medical school began Native Hawaiian cultural-competence courses in 1990 and founded a Department of Native Hawaiian Health in 2003. Today some clinics, such as Waimānalo Health Center, offer lāʻau lapaʻau, lomilomi and hoʻoponopono alongside primary care [3]. Honest accounts also record the fragility. One source notes that no living *poʻokela* (grand master) remains, and that Papa Auwae's lead student is the practitioner on track toward that level [10].

In Aotearoa, a national organisation of Māori traditional practitioners formed in 1993. From 1995, regional health authorities could purchase aspects of rongoā. The Ministry of Health published standards for traditional Māori healing in 1999 and a rongoā development plan in 2006 [4]. The knowledge that once had to hide now has a recognised place.

In Australia, tea tree oil sits on shelves worldwide. The most important change is less visible: Aboriginal communities increasingly decide for themselves what plant knowledge is shared, with whom, and on what terms.

## Threads to other traditions

- **Polynesian voyaging traditions → Hawaiian lāʻau lapaʻau** (the centuries of Polynesian settlement; exact dates debated). Canoe plants such as ʻawa, noni, kukui and ʻōlena were carried across the Pacific and became core healing plants in Hawaiʻi [3].
- **Bundjalung bush medicine → Australian chemistry → global commerce** (1920s onward). Traditional leaf use preceded Penfold's 1920s germicidal studies at Sydney's Technological Museum, after which a commercial tea tree oil industry grew, later revived with plantations in the 1970s and 1980s [1][5].
- **Australian flora → British science** (1788). First Fleet surgeon Denis Considen sent a sample of Sydney peppermint (*Eucalyptus piperita*) oil to Joseph Banks in England [5].
- **Christian missions and Western law → Hawaiian healing** (1820 onward; licensing board 1919–1959). Missionaries suppressed kahuna healing, and the licensing board required Western botanical names [3].
- **New Zealand state law → rongoā Māori** (1907–1962, then 1990s onward). Suppressed under the Tohunga Suppression Act, rongoā was later recognised in national health policy [4][6].
- **Western academic medicine ↔ Native Hawaiian health** (1990, 2003). The University of Hawaiʻi's medical school added Native Hawaiian cultural-competence teaching and a dedicated department [3].
