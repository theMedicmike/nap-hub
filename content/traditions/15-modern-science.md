# How modern medicine grew from all of these

*The pill in your cabinet has a family tree, and its oldest roots run through a willow by a river, a bark from the Andes and a recipe an old woman in Shropshire kept secret.*

## The legend (how the tradition tells its own beginning — clearly marked as tradition)

Every tradition in this book has a founding story. Modern scientific medicine says it has none. It likes to think it began the day people stopped believing and started measuring.

But it has legends too, and they are worth hearing as legends.

**The legend of the lone genius.** A brilliant man works alone, has a flash of insight, and the world changes. The real record is far more crowded: pharmacists' assistants, ship's surgeons, enslaved men, ambassadors' wives, rural families and ancient authors, many of whom never got their names on the paper.

**The legend of the Countess.** For centuries Europeans said that cinchona bark, the fever bark of the Andes, got its name from a Spanish countess of Chinchón who was healed by it in Peru and carried it home. It is a lovely story. Historians have long treated it as a legend rather than a record, and the scientific name *Cinchona* itself comes from the Swedish naturalist Linnaeus, who coined it in the 1700s with the story already in mind [4][5].

**The legend of the wise woman.** Another favourite tale says the English doctor William Withering learned about foxglove from "Mother Hutton," a Shropshire herbalist. Withering's own writings never name her, and neither does anyone else's from his time. The name and the picture of her appear to come from a 1928 advertising campaign by the drug company Parke-Davis [6]. What Withering actually wrote is quieter: he was asked about a recipe "long kept a secret by an old woman in Shropshire," and he never met her [6].

Even the science of evidence has its campfire stories.

## What the record shows (the oldest evidence, texts, dates, with honest dating debates)

The turning point was not a new plant. It was a new question. For thousands of years, healers asked *what has helped before?* From the 1700s onward, a growing number also asked *how do we know?* and then *what, exactly, inside this plant is doing it?*

**A test at sea, 1747.** On 20 May 1747, aboard the British warship *Salisbury*, the naval surgeon James Lind took twelve sailors who all had scurvy (a wasting illness of long voyages, with bleeding gums and deep tiredness). He split them into six pairs and gave each pair a different treatment then in use [9]. Lind published the result in Edinburgh in 1753 as *A Treatise of the Scurvy* [10]. Historians call it one of the earliest planned, controlled comparisons of treatments on record [9][10]. It was not neat, though. The trial fills only a few pages of a book of more than 450, and Lind left readers unsure what he actually recommended [9]. What it gave the world was a shape: treat like cases differently, side by side, and watch.

**From a remedy to a molecule.** The next leap happened in apothecary shops. In 1804, a young German apothecary's assistant named Friedrich Sertürner drew a crystalline substance out of raw opium. It was the first alkaloid ever isolated from any plant [1][2]. (An alkaloid is a nitrogen-containing compound that many plants make, often bitter and often strong in the body.) There is a dating wrinkle: a few sources say 1803. There is also a priority dispute. The Frenchman Armand Séguin described a similar result to the Académie des Sciences in 1804, but his work was not printed until 1814, so credit generally goes to Sertürner [3]. Sertürner's 1817 paper, which gave the substance the name "morphine," is the one that made scientists sit up [3].

After that, the old pharmacopeias (the official lists of medicines and how to make them) started to change. Plant after plant was taken apart:

- **1820:** quinine is pulled from cinchona bark in Paris [4][5].
- **1828:** salicin, the bitter principle of willow, is named by Johann Büchner. Earlier chemists had tried before him [7].
- **1897–1899:** acetylsalicylic acid is made in a stable form at Bayer and sold as "Aspirin" [7][8].
- **1958:** vinblastine is isolated from Madagascar periwinkle in Canada [12][13].
- **1970s:** artemisinin is drawn from *Artemisia annua*, sweet wormwood, in China [15][16].

**What about the famous statistic?** You will often see a claim that some exact share of today's medicines "came from plants" or "came from traditional knowledge." The figures in circulation differ widely, and they depend on definitions: a pure plant compound, a lab-modified one, or a molecule merely inspired by a natural shape. This chapter could not verify one figure well enough to print it. The honest version is this: a large and important part of the modern medicine chest traces back to natural sources, and some of those sources were first pointed out by traditional use.

## Keepers of the knowledge (named founders and great figures, each a vivid mini-portrait)

**Edward Stone, the parson who tasted a tree (1763).** An English clergyman tasted willow bark and was struck by its bitterness. He gave powdered willow bark to about fifty people with fevers and reported to the Royal Society in London in 1763 [7]. Careful historians add a footnote. When willow did not work, he sometimes added Peruvian bark, which muddies his results [7].

**William Withering, the doctor who counted (1775–1785).** In 1775, Withering was asked for his opinion on an old family recipe for dropsy (old name for painful swelling from fluid building up in the body). The mixture held about twenty herbs. Withering picked out purple foxglove, *Digitalis purpurea*, as the one that mattered [6]. He then spent about ten years testing it on patients before publishing *An Account of the Foxglove and Some of its Medicinal Uses* in 1785 [6]. Foxglove is also a poison, so careful dosing was the whole game.

**Friedrich Sertürner, the assistant who wouldn't stop (1804).** Picture a back room crowded with jars and glassware, and a young man coaxing pale crystals out of a dark, sticky opium paste. He tested the substance on dogs and then, riskily, on himself and a few young friends [1][2]. He named it after Morpheus, the Greek god of dreams [1]. Recognition came slowly. In 1831 the Institut de France gave him the Montyon Prize and the title "Benefactor of Humanity" [1].

**Pelletier and Caventou, the partners behind the shop (1820).** Two French pharmacists, Pierre-Joseph Pelletier and Joseph-Bienaimé Caventou, worked in a lab behind a Parisian apothecary shop. They isolated quinine from yellow cinchona bark. The same method on grey bark gave them a sister alkaloid, cinchonine [4][5]. By most accounts they did not patent the method. Firms soon took it up, and quinine was being made for sale [4]. Measured doses replaced a bark powder whose strength changed from batch to batch [4].

**Onesimus, the man who knew (early 1700s).** In Boston, an enslaved West African man named Onesimus told the minister Cotton Mather that he had been inoculated against smallpox as a child, back home in West Africa [11][17]. Exactly where home was is uncertain. Mather wrote down his people as "Guaramantee," which historians often read as Coromantee, a name linked to Akan-speaking peoples of the Gold Coast, today's Ghana [11]. Mather wrote to the Royal Society about the practice in 1716 [11]. When smallpox hit Boston in 1721, Mather and the physician Zabdiel Boylston pushed for inoculation against fierce public anger [11][17]. Onesimus's knowledge, carried across an ocean against his will, helped shape what came next.

**Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, the ambassador's wife (1717–1722).** She had survived smallpox herself, and it had scarred her. She arrived in Constantinople (now Istanbul) in 1717, where her husband was British ambassador, and watched women there inoculate children [17]. She had her young son inoculated there in 1718 and her daughter inoculated in England in 1721 [11][17]. In 1722 she encouraged the Princess of Wales to have her own daughters done [17].

**Edward Jenner, the country doctor (1796).** Jenner took material from a cowpox sore, from a mild disease of cattle, and put it into a boy named James Phipps. Later he exposed the boy to smallpox material, and the boy did not fall ill [11]. Jenner is often called the father of vaccination (the word comes from Latin *vacca*, "cow"). Historians stress that he stood on a much older inoculation tradition [11][17].

**Robert Noble and Charles Beer, the scientists who followed a tea (1950s).** A tea of periwinkle leaves was being used in Jamaica for diabetes. Leaves reached the University of Western Ontario through a physician's patient [12]. Accounts date the first interest to 1949 or 1952 [12][14]. The extracts did nothing against diabetes in rats. The research turned a corner instead, and in 1958 Noble and Beer isolated vinblastine. The related alkaloid vincristine was found as well [12][13][14]. Their colleague J. H. Cutts also worked on the discovery [12].

**Tu Youyou, the scientist who reread an old book (1970s).** Her team in China had screened many traditional preparations for a malaria medicine, at first without much luck. Tu went back to the sources. She found a line in *A Handbook of Prescriptions for Emergencies*, a work by the scholar and alchemist Ge Hong, who died around 343 CE [15][16]. It said to soak a handful of *qinghao* (sweet wormwood) in water, wring it out and drink the juice. Tu reasoned that heat might be destroying the active ingredient, so she switched to a low-temperature extraction. That was the breakthrough that led to artemisinin [15][16]. By 1973 her team had also made dihydroartemisinin, a stronger form [16]. In 2015 she shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with William C. Campbell and Satoshi Ōmura. She was the first Chinese citizen to win in that category [15][16][18]. Questions about how credit was shared inside the team have been debated, and Tu herself described the honour as belonging to her team and her country [15].

## How it was passed down (oral chant, verse, apprenticeship, manuscripts, schools — the generational chain)

The older traditions passed knowledge through memorised verse, a master's voice and manuscripts. Modern medicine kept the apprenticeship and added new links to the chain.

- **The apothecary shop.** Sertürner, Pelletier and Caventou learned pharmacy by hand, and their finds grew out of that craft [1][4].
- **The letter to the learned society.** Stone and Mather both wrote to the Royal Society in London, turning private observations into public, checkable claims [7][11].
- **The printed treatise.** Withering's *Account* and Lind's *Treatise* were books other doctors could buy, argue with and test [6][10].
- **The patent and the trade name.** "Aspirin" was a brand name, and Bayer's story of who invented it was later questioned. Historians have argued that Arthur Eichengrün directed the work Felix Hoffmann carried out [7][8]. Knowledge now passed through companies and courts as well as classrooms.
- **The ancient text, reread.** Tu Youyou's success shows the chain can also loop backward. A sentence written about sixteen centuries earlier could still steer a modern lab [15].

## How it traveled (routes, encounters, translations, conquests, what it gave and received)

Modern medicine was not born in one country. It was put together from imports.

- **From the Andes to Europe.** Andean peoples knew the fever bark first [4]. Jesuit missionaries carried it to Europe, where it was sold as "Jesuit's powder" or "Peruvian bark" from about the 1630s [4][5]. This happened inside the Spanish conquest of the region, and the people who first knew the tree were rarely credited.
- **Across Asia, Africa and the Ottoman world.** Historians agree that smallpox inoculation began somewhere in Asia, in China or India, and possibly in both [17]. Chinese written records of blowing powdered smallpox scabs into the nose date to the 1500s; later Chinese tradition placed it centuries earlier, but that claim is not documented from the time [11]. Claims of very early Indian practice are disputed, and one 2012 study argued there is no firm basis for them [11]. The practice was also known in the Ottoman Empire and in parts of Africa, and historians cannot always say which way it travelled [11]. From Constantinople it reached London through Lady Mary. From West Africa it reached Boston through Onesimus [11][17]. In the late 1760s Catherine the Great of Russia was inoculated by the English doctor Thomas Dimsdale, along with her son, Grand Duke Paul [11][17].
- **From the Caribbean to Canada.** Jamaican tea knowledge travelled north in an envelope of leaves and became a laboratory project [12][14].
- **From a fourth-century Chinese handbook to the world.** Artemisinin left China and became part of global malaria treatment. It is the clearest recent case of an ancient text guiding a modern drug [15][16].

What modern medicine gave back was mostly a method. It also took a great deal without credit, and that belongs to its history too.

## Signature ideas and plants (the worldview in plain English; key plants as history, never as cures)

**The big idea in one sentence:** *Don't just ask what helped; find out exactly what is acting, how much of it, and whether it does better than the alternative.*

Three habits grew from it: **isolate** the single active substance rather than use a whole plant whose strength shifts with soil and season; **standardise the dose**; and **compare fairly**, as Lind's six pairs first did [4][6][9].

**The plants, as history:**

- **Willow (*Salix*)** → salicin (1828) → acetylsalicylic acid, sold as Aspirin (1899) [7][8]. Willow-bark remedies are described in very old records. Some accounts trace them to ancient Sumer and Egypt [7].
- **Cinchona** → quinine (1820), from Andean knowledge by way of Jesuit trade [4][5].
- **Foxglove (*Digitalis purpurea*)** → digitalis medicines, from an old woman's recipe and ten years of careful case notes [6].
- **Opium poppy** → morphine (1804), the first plant alkaloid ever isolated [1][2].
- **Sweet wormwood (*Artemisia annua*)** → artemisinin, from Ge Hong's handbook by way of Tu Youyou's lab [15][16].
- **Madagascar periwinkle (*Catharanthus roseus*, once called *Vinca rosea*)** → vinblastine and vincristine. A diabetes folk remedy that failed its test led to a different and unexpected result [12][14].

A caution belongs here, and the history itself teaches it. In every one of these cases, the medicine was **not** the plant. Each was a purified, measured, tested compound, and several of these plants are toxic in the wrong amount. Traditional use pointed the way. It was never the proof.

## Today (where it lives now, honestly)

Modern scientific medicine is now the main system in hospitals worldwide, and the old pharmacopeias still echo inside it. Descendants of willow, poppy, cinchona, foxglove, periwinkle and sweet wormwood all have places in today's medicine. Each is judged on clinical evidence, not on where it came from.

Two honest tensions remain. The first is **credit**. The people who first knew cinchona, inoculation or periwinkle tea were seldom named and seldom paid, and who benefits from traditional knowledge is still argued over. The second is **humility**. Tu Youyou's story shows the oldest books can still hold good questions, if someone reads them carefully and tests what they say.

## Threads to other traditions (bullets: what it gave to / received from which tradition, when)

- **Received from Andean Indigenous knowledge:** cinchona bark, carried to Europe by Jesuits from about the 1630s and purified to quinine in 1820 [4][5].
- **Received from Chinese medicine:** smallpox inoculation by nasal powder, recorded from the 1500s [11]. Also *qinghao* and Ge Hong's soaking method (c. 4th century CE), which led to artemisinin in the 1970s [15][16].
- **Received from Ottoman practice:** inoculation through a small cut in the skin, seen in Constantinople in 1717 and brought to England by 1721 [17].
- **Received from West African practice:** inoculation knowledge carried by Onesimus to Boston, recorded by 1716 and put to use in 1721 [11][17].
- **Received from European folk and herbal medicine:** the foxglove family recipe (1775) and the willow-bark tradition behind Stone's 1763 report [6][7].
- **Received from Caribbean folk medicine:** Jamaican periwinkle tea, which led to vinblastine in 1958 [12][14].
- **Received from the Greek and Arabic pharmacy tradition, through the apothecary trade:** the shop, the pharmacopeia and the craft of preparing drugs where Sertürner, Pelletier and Caventou trained [1][4].
- **Gave to all traditions:** the controlled comparison (Lind, 1747), the isolated active compound (Sertürner, 1804), and the standardised dose [1][4][9].
